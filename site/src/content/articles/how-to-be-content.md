Contentment is usually pictured as a temperament: some people are easily pleased and the
rest of us are not. [Paul](/figure/paul/) writes, "I have learned to be content regardless of my
circumstances" (Philippians 4:11). Learned, and he says so twice in two verses. A thing
that was learned can be learned by someone else.

## What Paul says he learned

Paul is in chains (Philippians 1:13), writing to thank the Philippians for the gifts they
sent (Philippians 4:18), and he is careful that the thanks is not a hint for more: "I am
not saying this out of need" (Philippians 4:11). Then the claim: "In any and every
situation I have learned the secret of being filled and being hungry, of having plenty and
having need" (Philippians 4:12).

Plenty is on the list. Paul treats abundance
as a second thing to be learned, and Ecclesiastes says why: "He who loves money is never
satisfied by money" (Ecclesiastes 5:10).

The famous next line belongs to this paragraph: "I can do all things through Christ who
gives me strength" (Philippians 4:13). In context the "all things" are being hungry and
being full. It is a sentence about a bad month and a good one.

## What it rests on

Hebrews gives the command and the reason in one breath. The command: "Keep your lives free
from the love of money and be content with what you have". The reason is a promise: "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5). The ground of
contentment is not that you have enough. It is that you have not been left, which is why
it can survive a change in what you have.

Paul warns Timothy about teachers who "regard godliness as a means of gain"
(1 Timothy 6:5). His reply: "godliness with contentment is great gain" (1 Timothy 6:6). We
brought nothing in and carry nothing out (1 Timothy 6:7), so "if we have food and
clothing, we will be content with these" (1 Timothy 6:8). The floor he names is low.

## The barn

Asked to settle an inheritance dispute, Jesus declines (Luke 12:13–14) and says instead
that "one’s life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15). Then
a story: a farmer has a harvest too big for his barns, plans bigger ones and years of
ease, and dies that night. The verdict falls not on the harvest but on a man "who stores
up treasure for himself but is not rich toward God" (Luke 12:21). In the story the farmer
talks only to himself.

## Three practices

**Ask for a portion, not a pile.** The prayer in Proverbs 30:8 is exact: "Give me neither
poverty nor riches; feed me with the bread that is my portion." The next verse gives the
reason, and it cuts both ways: too much and I may deny God, too little and I may steal
(Proverbs 30:9). The Lord's Prayer asks on the same scale, for "daily bread"
(Matthew 6:11).

**Name the want.** The commandment against coveting is a list: a house, a wife, servants,
an ox, a donkey, "or anything that belongs to your neighbor" (Exodus 20:17). Discontent is
rarely general. It has a house, a salary or a marriage in view, usually someone else's.
Say which one, in prayer, as plainly as the commandment does.

**Give some away.** Paul's instruction for the rich is "to be generous and ready to
share," with their hope set on God, "who richly provides all things for us to enjoy"
(1 Timothy 6:17–18). Giving shows whether you own a thing or it owns you.

## What it does not mean

It is not pretending, and it is not giving up. Paul was content in chains and still called
the Philippians' gift a good thing: "you have done well to share in my affliction"
(Philippians 4:14). Fix what can be fixed. The question is whether your peace is waiting
on the repair.

Christians differ on how far this goes. When Jesus tells the rich young man to "sell your
possessions and give to the poor" (Matthew 19:21), Catholic and Orthodox Christians have
long heard a call that some take up literally, as monks and nuns do by vow. Protestant
teaching has generally heard a command to that man about his particular idol, and locates
contentment inside ordinary work and ownership. Both hold to Hebrews 13:5.
