"Do not worry" is among the best known things Jesus said and among the least useful when
quoted alone. Being told to stop rarely stops anyone. The passages themselves do more
than that: they say what the worry is about, why it
does not work, and what to do with it instead.

## What Jesus was talking about

Matthew 6:25 begins with "Therefore", so it depends on the verse before it: "You cannot
serve both God and money" (Matthew 6:24). The worry in view is specific. It is about
provision: "what you will eat or drink; or about your body, what you will wear"
(Matthew 6:25).

Jesus gives reasons, not just an order. The first is that worry accomplishes nothing: "Who
of you by worrying can add a single hour to his life?" (Matthew 6:27). The second is that
the need is already known: "your heavenly Father knows that you need them" (Matthew 6:32).
He does not say the needs are unreal.

Then the last line: "Therefore do not worry about
tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself. Today has enough trouble of its own"
(Matthew 6:34). That is not a promise of an easy week. It concedes trouble and puts a
boundary on it. Today's is yours to deal with. Tomorrow's is not here.

## Concern is not the problem

The word [Paul](/figure/paul/) uses when he writes "Be anxious for nothing"
(Philippians 4:6) belongs to the
same Greek word family he uses, approvingly, two chapters earlier. Timothy is commended as
someone "who will genuinely care for your needs" (Philippians 2:20). Paul lists among his
own burdens "the pressure of my concern for all the churches" (2 Corinthians 11:28), and he
is not confessing a fault.

So Scripture does not ask for indifference. The warning is against care that has come
loose from trust.

Luke's account of [Martha](/figure/martha/) shows what that looks like. She is doing good
work, hosting Jesus,
and He tells her, "you are worried and upset about many things. But only one thing is
necessary" (Luke 10:41-42). The contrast is between many things and one, and the one is
what her sister Mary was doing: sitting "at the Lord’s feet listening to His message"
(Luke 10:39).

## What to do with it

Paul's instruction is a replacement, not a suppression: "Be anxious for nothing, but in
everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God"
(Philippians 4:6). The concern is not denied. It is turned into a request and handed over.
He wrote this as a prisoner, "in chains for Christ" (Philippians 1:13), so it is not advice
from a comfortable man.

Verse 7 does not say the request will be granted. It says "the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your
minds in Christ Jesus." The guard is posted over the person, not over the outcome.

[Peter](/figure/peter/) gives the same instruction with a different verb: "Cast all your
anxiety on Him, because He
cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7). The verse before it reads "Humble yourselves, therefore, under
God’s mighty hand" (1 Peter 5:6), and the two belong together. Worry often assumes that
everything depends on you. Handing it over admits that it does not.

## Is worry a sin?

Christians answer differently. Some read "do not worry" as a command like any other, so that
worry is a failure to trust and something to confess. Others hear Jesus reassuring
frightened people, and treat worry as a weakness to be carried to God more than a
transgression. Both readings take the same remedy from the texts, and both hear the same
reason given for it: "your heavenly Father knows that you need them" (Matthew 6:32).

Persistent anxiety can also be a medical matter, and seeing a doctor about it is not a
failure of trust. Scripture does not treat distress as disgrace. In
Gethsemane Jesus "began to be sorrowful and deeply distressed" (Matthew 26:37), and the
psalmist writes "When anxiety overwhelms me" (Psalm 94:19) as a thing that happens to him.

## Four things to do with a worry

**Turn each worry into one sentence of request.** Out loud or on paper.

**Add one particular thanks to it.** Philippians 4:6 puts thanksgiving inside the asking.

**Sort it by day.** If something can be done today, do that. If it belongs to tomorrow,
Matthew 6:34 says it can stay there.

**Tell one person.** "Anxiety weighs down the heart of a man, but a good word cheers it up"
(Proverbs 12:25).
