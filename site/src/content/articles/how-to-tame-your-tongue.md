Most advice about speech treats it as a matter of technique: count to ten, choose better
words. James is blunter. "All kinds of animals, birds, reptiles, and creatures of the sea
are being tamed and have been tamed by man, but no man can tame the tongue" (James 3:7–8).
So the title of this article promises something Scripture says nobody can deliver. That
changes the kind of work this is.

## Why James takes it so seriously

James 3 argues from small things that steer large ones: a bit in a horse's mouth, a rudder
on a ship, and then, "Consider how small a spark sets a great forest ablaze" (James 3:5).
A sentence takes four seconds to say and can set the direction of
a friendship for years.

He is not writing about other people. "We all stumble in many ways" (James 3:2), and the
chapter opens with a warning to teachers. His sharpest
complaint is inconsistency: "With the tongue we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we
curse men, who have been made in God's likeness" (James 3:9). "My brothers, this should not be!" (James 3:10).

Yet the same letter says that anyone who "does not bridle his tongue" has a religion that
is "worthless" (James 1:26). A bridle does not change the horse; it
holds it, daily.

## Where words come from

Jesus locates the problem further back than the mouth: "For out of the overflow of the
heart the mouth speaks" (Luke 6:45). Speech is a readout. James ends his argument in the
same place, with springs and fig trees (James 3:11–12).

What you say when tired or cornered is better information than what you
say when composed. And the long repair is upstream. A grievance
rehearsed in private for a month will find its way into a sentence.

So the psalmist asks for help: "Set a
guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch at the door of my lips" (Psalm 141:3). If no man
can tame the tongue, that is the sensible place to begin.

## Four practices

**Listen first.** "Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger"
(James 1:19). The order is the instruction. Proverbs is less polite: "He who
answers a matter before he hears it—this is folly and disgrace to him" (Proverbs 18:13).
In your next disagreement, state the other person's position to their satisfaction before
you answer it.

**Say less.** "When words are many, sin is unavoidable, but he who restrains his lips is
wise" (Proverbs 10:19). This is arithmetic, not a rule of silence. The easiest sentences to drop are the clever remark, the last word, and the correction nobody needed.

**Give every sentence a job.** "Let no unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only
what is helpful for building up the one in need and bringing grace to those who listen"
(Ephesians 4:29). A few verses earlier [Paul](/figure/paul/) requires truthful speech
(Ephesians 4:25), so
this is no licence for flattery, and a hard word can pass. A true remark made in order to wound cannot.

**Be no fuel.** "Without wood, a fire goes out; without gossip, a conflict ceases"
(Proverbs 26:20). Gossip needs a second person. You can decline to be one.

## A plain yes

Jesus says, "Simply let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No.'" (Matthew 5:37), and James
gives the same instruction (James 5:12). Christians read the reach of this differently. Anabaptists
(Schleitheim Confession, article 7) and the Society of Friends take it to forbid every
oath, courtroom included, and affirm instead. Catholic, Anglican and Reformed standards
(Catechism of the Catholic Church, 2154; Thirty-Nine Articles, XXXIX; Westminster
Confession, chapter 22) take it to forbid rash and needless swearing while allowing an
oath for a grave reason, as in court. Both readings agree on the everyday
case: your ordinary word should hold without reinforcement. Notice how often you say
"honestly" or "I swear", and what that implies about the rest.

## What to expect

You will fail at this; James 3:2 says so in advance. That is no reason to shrug. Jesus
warns that people "will give an account on the day of judgment for every careless word
they have spoken" (Matthew 12:36). He is answering Pharisees who had just slandered His
work, but it is the careless word He names. Progress tends to look like a shorter gap between the remark and the apology, and
later a pause before the remark.
