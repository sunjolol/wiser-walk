Sometime in the 300s, a man named Marcellinus was getting over an illness. He spent the
time with the Psalms, trying to understand each one, and his friend Athanasius, the bishop
of Alexandria, heard about it from the man who carried his letter. Athanasius wrote back
with what an old man, a lover of study, had once taught him about the Psalms, holding the
book in his hands.

The letter is short and practical, and the Church has kept it close. In the Codex
Alexandrinus, one of the oldest Bibles in the world, copied in the 400s, it stands in front
of the Book of Psalms as the way in. Here is what it says to do.

## Say them as your own words

Most of the Bible, the old man said, you read as words about other people (§11). Nobody
takes Moses's plea to be blotted out of God's book for his people's sake and prays it as
their own, and nobody borrows Elijah's oath about the Lord before Whom he stood. We read
those as theirs.

The Psalms are different. Apart from their prophecies of Christ, whoever says them says
them as his own words, boldly, as though they had been written about him. Then comes the
image the letter is known for: the Psalms become a kind of mirror to the one who sings
them, in which he sees himself and the movements of his own soul (§12).

So when you pray Psalm 3 with your own troubles in view, its words are yours:

> "O LORD, how my foes have increased! How many rise up against me!" (Psalm 3:1)

Say "I" and mean yourself. Put your own situation behind the words and let the psalm say
it for you.

## Let them give you the words

The rest of Scripture tells you what to do. The Psalms also tell you what to say while you
do it (§10). You are told to repent; the Psalms show you how, and what to say when you do.
You are told that trouble produces endurance; the Psalms show how to bear trouble, what to
say in the middle of it and what to say afterwards. You are told to give thanks in
everything; the Psalms give you the words to give thanks with.

This is the most useful thing in the letter for anyone who has sat down to pray and found
nothing to say. You do not have to invent a prayer. You can borrow one that has been
prayed for some three thousand years:

> "Have mercy on me, O God, according to Your loving devotion; according to Your great
> compassion, blot out my transgressions." (Psalm 51:1)

## Choose the psalm for what you are in

Then the old man went through life one situation at a time and named a psalm for each. A
few of them:

- If your own people have turned on you and more keep rising against you, [Psalm 3](/psalm/3/) (§15).
- If it has gone on so long you wonder whether God has forgotten you, [Psalm 13](/psalm/13/) (§16).
- If you can see the Lord shepherding you and guiding your way, [Psalm 23](/psalm/23/) (§17).
- If you have done wrong and want to come back, [Psalm 51](/psalm/51/) (§20).
- If life has worn you down until you feel empty, and you need comfort, [Psalm 102](/psalm/102/) (§24).

His picture for this is a garden. Take from the Psalms, as you would take fruit from a
garden, whatever you see you need (§30). He believed the whole of human life is measured
out in them, every state of the soul and every movement of thought, and that nothing in
people is found beyond them (§30).

All 53 of his situations are in [one guide](/psalm/), each with its psalm printed in full.
If you are not sure which is yours, [a few honest questions](/q/which-psalm/) will find it.

## Leave the words as they are

On this the letter is firm (§31). Do not dress the Psalms up in clever words from outside
them, and do not try to reword them or change them. Say them and sing them simply, as
written.

His reason is the most striking line in the letter: so that the holy men who first spoke
them may recognise their own words and pray with you, and, more than that, so that the
Spirit Who spoke in them, hearing His own words, may help you. As much as the lives of the
saints were better than ours, he adds, their words are better and stronger than anything
we could put together (§31).

Your own words still have a place. Start with theirs.

## Sing them, or at least say them aloud

Why are the Psalms sung at all? Not for the pleasure of the sound, the old man said (§27).
A person is full of different movements, reasoning, desire and temper, and they are meant
to agree rather than pull apart. He gives Pilate as the warning: Pilate judged rightly that
he found no fault in Jesus, then acted against his own judgment and gave in to the crowd.
Singing a psalm in tune is a picture of a soul in order, and it helps put the soul in order
(§§27–29).

Then he shows how it works, with three lines (§28). What is troubled and grieving in you is
smoothed out and healed as you sing:

> "Why are you downcast, O my soul? Why the unease within me?" (Psalm 42:5)

What has slipped recognises itself as it says:

> "But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled; my steps had nearly slipped." (Psalm 73:2)

And what is afraid is strengthened by hope as it says:

> "The LORD is on my side; I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?" (Psalm 118:6)

People who sing this way, he says, sing with the tongue and with the mind, and they help
not only themselves but whoever hears them (§29). His example is David playing for Saul:
"whenever the spirit from God came upon Saul, David would pick up his harp and play. Then
Saul would find relief and feel better" (1 Samuel 16:23).

You do not need a trained voice. A psalm said slowly and out loud keeps much of the same
rhythm, and it keeps your attention on the words.

## Pray them for someone else

Near the end, the letter turns outward (§33). If you care for someone who is suffering,
say these words for them. It helps the one who suffers, and it shows your own faith to be
real. Athanasius's psalm for someone you love who is in trouble is [Psalm 20](/psalm/20/)
(§17):

> "May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble; may the name of the God of Jacob protect
> you." (Psalm 20:1)

## Keep at it

He closes with Psalm 119, where the writer says the Scriptures carried him: "Your statutes
are songs to me in the house of my pilgrimage" (Psalm 119:54), and "If Your law had not
been my delight, then I would have perished in my affliction" (Psalm 119:92). So, he tells
Marcellinus, keep studying them and read them with understanding. Guided by the Spirit, you
will grasp the meaning in each psalm (§33), which is what Marcellinus wanted in the first
place.

*Bible text: Berean Standard Bible. Athanasius's letter is paraphrased from his Greek
(Migne, Patrologia Graeca 27), with its paragraph numbers.*
