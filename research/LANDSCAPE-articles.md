# Landscape: Christian formation writing, and what wiserwalk's articles should become

Researched 2026-09-19. Every outside claim below carries the URL where it was read. Where a page
refused to load, it says so. Nothing here is a traffic estimate; the only demand evidence used is
what publishers print about themselves.

---

## 1. The three findings that matter most

1. **The live gratitude article makes a claim that Scripture and a whole tradition contradict, and
   it does not say so.** It says: "In all circumstances" is not "for all circumstances". But
   Ephesians 5:20 in the site's own bundled BSB reads "always giving thanks to God the Father for
   everything". The Orthodox priest Stephen Freeman builds an entire, widely read piece on exactly
   that verse, arguing thanks FOR all things, not merely IN them
   (http://www.glory2godforallthings.com/2011/02/09/the-difficult-path-of-giving-thanks/). On a site
   whose moat is fairness, this is the kind of thing that gets screenshotted. Fix: keep the
   1 Thessalonians reading, add Ephesians 5:20, and say plainly that Christians read the pair
   differently.
2. **The demand is for verses and for fears, not for virtues.** YouVersion says its most searched
   terms in 2025 were love, anxiety and peace, and its top verses Isaiah 41:10, Jeremiah 29:11 and
   Romans 12:2 (https://www.youversion.com/news/youversion-announces-2025-verse-of-the-year). Bible
   Gateway's 2025 top verse was Psalm 23:4, with Psalm 23 and Psalm 91 filling most of the top
   places and Jeremiah 29:11 at seventh
   (https://religionunplugged.com/news/2025/12/9/here-are-the-most-read-bible-verses-of-2025).
   BibleProject has visibly reorganised its article section around this: its featured pieces are
   "What Does Psalm 23 Mean?", "What Does Jeremiah 29:11 Mean?", "What Does 'Faith Without Works Is
   Dead' Mean?" (https://bibleproject.com/articles/). "What does this verse actually mean" is the
   article shape that is both searchable and a natural partner to Sounds Like Scripture and Finish
   the Verse. The site has none yet.
3. **The three live articles were written before the site's own verification pipeline and do not
   meet it.** The ten drafts in `audit/article-drafts/` quote the BSB verbatim, script-checked. The
   three live articles quote a different translation without naming it (1 Thessalonians 5:18 live:
   "give thanks in all circumstances"; BSB: "Give thanks in every circumstance"). The wording
   matches the ESV as I know it, which is a copyrighted text with a quotation policy; I did not open
   an ESV page to confirm. They also cross-link to `seven-deadly-sins`, which CLAUDE.md says stays
   draft and out of every shared object until audited. Re-base all three on the BSB and re-point the
   links before writing anything new.

### What the best do that the mills do not

- **One passage read in its setting beats nine verses listed.** BibleProject spends 1,600 words on
  one verse. Crosswalk spends 1,100 on nine.
- **They name the misreading first.** The reader arrives holding the greeting-card version; the
  piece starts there.
- **They answer the objection inside the piece** (Willard on hedonism; Freeman on "how can I thank
  God for evil?").
- **They quote the dead, by name and work.** Tarrants cites seven writers across sixteen centuries.
  A mill cites "studies".
- **They name the translation.**
- **They end small.** A synthesis or an admission, never "5 takeaways" and never a product.

### Length, as observed

Mill list piece about 1,100; Freeman about 1,200; BibleProject verse piece about 1,600; Willard
excerpt about 1,800; CSLI essay about 4,500. The site's drafts are held to 650 to 800 words. That
is fine for a "how to become more X" piece on a phone, and too short for a "what does this verse
mean" piece, which needs the setting, the misreading and the traditions. Recommendation: two
formats. **Practice pieces 700 to 900 words. Passage pieces 1,200 to 1,600.**

## 4. House style guide for articles (under 400 words)

**Who it is for.** Someone on a phone who typed a question. Answer it in the first hundred words,
then earn the rest.

**Two formats.** *Practice piece* ("How to become more patient"): 700 to 900 words. *Passage piece*
("What does Jeremiah 29:11 mean?"): 1,200 to 1,600 words. Nothing longer.

**Opening.** Start with what the reader already believes or has been told, then say what is wrong
or thin about it. No anecdotes about the author. No "In today's busy world".

**Scripture.** Quote the BSB, verbatim, machine-checked, and name it once at the foot. Every
quotation carries its reference. Read each verse with the verses around it and say who was being
addressed. One passage explained beats five listed. Never cite a verse for a claim it does not
make. If a practical tip has no passage under it, say it is practical wisdom.

**Other voices.** At least one named writer from before 1900, with work and chapter, quoted from a
public-domain text. Draw from across the church over the series: Fathers, medievals, Reformers,
Puritans, Wesley, Orthodox and Catholic writers. Pre-1054 Fathers are claimed for nobody.

**Fairness.** If traditions read the passage differently, say so in one short section titled
"Where Christians differ", each view in words its holders would accept, cited to a confession,
catechism or standard work. Never settle it. Link to the Compass axis. No tradition is "some
people".

**No invented facts.** No statistics without a link. No "studies show". No claims about what most
Christians feel or do.

**Pastoral floor.** Any piece touching mood, fear, grief, anger or compulsion carries a plain
paragraph: this may be medical; see a doctor; bring it to your pastor or priest. Count knowledge,
never devotion: no streaks, no "do this for 30 days".

**Voice.** Plain verbs, short sentences, second person. No church-speak ("season", "journey",
"lean in", "unpack"). No exclamation marks. No flattery of the reader, no belittling of anyone.
British spelling.

**Structure.** Four to six H2s that read as a summary when skimmed. Bold lead-ins for lists.
Headline is the question as typed. No numbered-listicle headlines.

**Ending.** End small: one thing to try, or one honest limit. Then one cross-link to a quiz or
game and one to a sibling article. Then Sources and a "last reviewed" date. Corrections go to the
GitHub issue intake.
