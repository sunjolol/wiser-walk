# Landscape: the best Christian quizzes and self-assessments

Researched 2026-09-19 for wiserwalk.com. Lens: quizzes and self-assessments only (games and articles are other lenses).

**How to read this.** "Observed" means I opened the page and read it through a fetch tool that returns the page text. "Claims" means the site says it about itself. "Could not verify" means the page blocked me or the fact was not on the page. I did not take any quiz to the end, so result screens are described only where the landing page describes them. No traffic figures are given anywhere in this file, because none of these sites publish them and I will not guess.

---

## 1. The three findings that matter most

1. **The whole spiritual-gifts category is weak on the one thing this site is strong at.** Every big gifts test is a long list of "how true is this of me" self-ratings (66 to 125 items), most take your name and email before or after, and the academic literature says plainly that these inventories have almost no published reliability or validity work behind them. One factor-analytic study of a 14-gift inventory found three factors, not fourteen. Nobody in the top results says this to the user. A test that is ungated, shorter, honest about what a self-rating can and cannot show, and clear about which gift list it uses (and lets a cessationist and a charismatic each take it without being insulted) has no real rival.

2. **The "which Bible character / which saint are you" category is entirely arbitrary, and everyone knows it.** The big ones are Playbuzz or quiz-farm embeds (Catholic-Link's is literally a Playbuzz iframe; Cru lists "Which Bible character" beside "Who's your '90s TV dad"). Questions are favourite-colour style and the mapping to a figure is invented. They spread anyway because the result is about *me* and is showable. That is proof of appetite and a warning: the appetite is for the result, and the honest version is hard. See the concepts section for ways to get a personal, showable result with **zero authored opinion items**.

3. **There is a new direct competitor to the Compass, and it has a community.** TheoCompass (theocompass.com, by a Reddit user, open beta) matches you to "230+" denominations across 13 dimensions, with its own subreddit and a Ko-fi. Its landing page shows no sources. The Compass's edge is exactly what is already built: eighteen audited tradition families, positions in holders' own words, a published changelog and disputes list. The thing to take from TheoCompass is the front door: people type "what denomination am I", not "theology compass".

## 2. The fifteen that matter most

### Spiritual gifts tests

**1. SpiritualGiftsTest.com** — https://spiritualgiftstest.com/
- Who: Jeff Carver, a pastor in Southern California (from the site's About page as shown in search snippets; the site returned HTTP 403 to my fetches, so I could not read the pages myself).
- How it works: adult and youth versions; the youth test is 77 questions (search snippet of the site's own page). Adult length could not be verified.
- Claims: founded 2004; "over 1.5 million people" have taken the tests in ten years. **Read only in a search snippet of the About page, not on the page itself.**
- Good: exact-match domain, a youth version (youth pastors are a real distribution channel), downloadable PDFs for group use.
- Weak: could not inspect gating or item quality.
- Spread: exact-match domain in search; churches print and link it.

**2. GiftsTest.com** — https://giftstest.com/ and https://giftstest.com/test
- Who: no operator named anywhere I could see; footer reads "© 2026 GiftsTest.com".
- Observed: 66 items, five-point Never-to-Always scale. **First name, last name and email are asked for before the test starts.** The home page says results are private unless you ask otherwise.
- Good: short, clean, ranks first or second for the head term.
- Weak: anonymous operator plus mandatory email is a poor trust posture; no statement of which gift list or theology it follows on the landing page.
- Rock Church San Diego runs what appears to be the same 66-item, 1-to-5 format at https://www.sdrock.com/giftstest and says results "go in church database" — that is the real business model of the category: volunteer placement.

**3. ChurchGrowth.org "Team Ministry" survey** — https://gifts.churchgrowth.org/spiritual-gifts-survey/
- Who: Church Growth Institute; survey by Larry Gilbert, "recently revised".
- Observed: 108 statements about "feelings and desires"; nine "team" or task gifts only (evangelism, prophecy, teaching, exhortation, shepherding, serving, mercy, giving, administration); result is a bar graph plus pages of printed analysis; a Group Edition lets a church collect everyone's results.
- Claims: "Millions of people have taken this survey" (observed on https://gifts.churchgrowth.org/). A search summary said "over 8 million"; **I did not see that number on a page I opened.**
- Good: honest about scope — it deliberately measures nine task gifts and leaves the sign gifts out, and the group edition is why pastors send whole congregations to it.
- Weak: 108 items is long; account and login machinery; dated design.
- Spread: pastor sends link to congregation. This is the dominant channel for the whole category.

**4. Lifeway Spiritual Gifts Survey (PDF)** — https://www.lifeway.com/en/articles/women-leadership-spiritual-gifts-growth-service
- Who: Lifeway Christian Resources (Southern Baptist publishing arm).
- How: a downloadable PDF, 80 statements on a five-point scale, hand-scored (length and scale from Lifeway's own PDF text as surfaced in search; the PDF itself did not parse in my tool). Lifeway describes the items as a mix of actions, traits and statements of belief.
- Good: free, no gate on the PDF that I could see, and it is mirrored on dozens of church sites (Florida UMC, local churches) — a PDF a church can photocopy travels further than any web app.
- Weak: paper scoring in 2026; mixing "what I do", "what I am like" and "what I believe" in one scale is a measurement muddle.
- Lesson: **a printable, photocopiable version is a distribution feature, not a legacy.**

**5. WhatAreMySpiritualGifts.org** — https://www.whataremyspiritualgifts.org/
- Who: Denison Ministries (Denison Forum).
- Observed: 85 items, five-point scale; 17 gifts including music and hospitality; **explicitly excludes the sign gifts (healing, miracles, tongues)**; results are graphed and emailed.
- Good: it states its theological premise out loud ("passions and opportunities indicate" gifts) and says what it leaves out. That is the most honest framing I found.
- Weak: the exclusion is a position presented as a given; a Pentecostal taking it is simply not represented. Email delivery.

**6. MinTools basic test** — https://mintools.com/spiritual-gifts-test.htm
- Who: Ministry Tools Resource Center.
- Observed: 35 statements, seven Romans 12 "motivational" gifts only, scored in the browser, no email. It tells the user the test cannot show a full gift mix and that they may well have other gifts.
- Good: the shortest, least gated, most candid of the lot. Sells a fuller paid resource behind it.
- Weak: 1990s web page; seven gifts only.

**7. Church-run long inventories: North Point "My Gifts"** — https://northpointministries.org/dream-job-gifts-assessment
- Observed: 125 questions, 0-to-4 scale, "20-45 minutes", open access; instructs people to answer from actual experience, not ideals, and quickly.
- Good: the instruction to rate past experience rather than aspiration is the single best item-writing idea in the category.
- Weak: 125 questions on a phone is a wall. Exists to staff volunteer teams.

**Related, shorter notes:** Saddleback's SHAPE (gifts, heart, abilities, personality, experiences) is a class-length exercise of 45–90 minutes, now resold by church-software vendors who pitch searchable results for volunteer placement (https://ministryvitals.com/what-is-the-shape-test-at-saddleback-church/). The Catholic equivalent is the Catherine of Siena Institute's Called & Gifted process, which covers 24 charisms and places the inventory inside a workshop and a discernment interview rather than online alone (per diocesan pages in search results; siena.org returned 403 to me). Alan Hirsch's APEST fivefold test is 50 forced-choice pairs, about ten minutes, paid (about $10 per a third-party page; his own site now sits behind a login, so **price not verified**). Forced-choice pairs are worth noting: they cannot be gamed by agreeing with everything.

**What the research says about all of the above.** A 1989 study in the *Journal of Psychology and Theology* (Ledbetter and Foster) factor-analysed a 14-gift inventory and got poor-to-moderate reliabilities and three factors. A 2013 review in the *Journal of Psychology and Christianity* (Horvath, "Spiritual Gifts Inventories: A Psychometric Perspective") notes few published reliability or validity studies and no test of convergent validity. I read these as abstracts and search summaries only (the journal pages returned 403), so treat the detail as **secondary**: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/009164718901700309 and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255710212.

### Denomination and belief quizzes

**8. TheoCompass** — https://theocompass.com/
- Who: "Oroq", a Reddit user; community at r/TheoCompass; Ko-fi donations.
- Observed: open beta, 30 questions now, 60 and 120-question modes planned (an independent blogger took a 63-question version in February 2026: https://forestandfaith.org/2026/02/02/which-theology-theocompass-v1-0/). 13 axes, "230+" denominations, a compass chart, a "theological fingerprint", a label cloud, side-by-side denomination comparison. It also scores certainty and posture toward others, which is a clever idea. Slogan: built for informed decision, not persuasion.
- Good: ambition, community feedback loop, ungated, the certainty dimension.
- Weak: no sources on the landing page; 230 denominations positioned on 13 axes by one person is an enormous unaudited editorial surface; the blogger called it made "just for fun".
- What a rigorous version does differently: exactly what the Compass already does — fewer traditions, each position cited and written in holders' words, with a public disputes log.

**9. DenominationDifferences.com quiz, and the quiz-farm versions**
- https://denominationdifferences.com/quiz — observed: 50-plus multiple-choice questions where each option is a denomination's stated position; skip allowed; result is a percentage match table; ad slots in the page; no operator named. Includes LDS, Jehovah's Witnesses, Adventists, Quakers, Messianic Judaism. It sits on a site of comparison pages, which is how it gets found.
- GotoQuiz (https://www.gotoquiz.com/which_christian_denomination_do_you_belong_to): 16 questions, nine result buckets, a hobbyist author, 121 comments — people argue with their result in public, which is a form of spread.
- HowStuffWorks and ProProfs have versions too (HowStuffWorks returned an empty page to my tool).
- Good: picking among real positions beats agree/disagree scales for this topic.
- Weak: percentage match (the Compass rightly refuses this framing for people, and hedges it for traditions), ads, no citations, answers that tip the denomination by vocabulary.

### Character, saint and personality

**10. "Which Bible character are you?" (Beliefnet, Cru, Catholic-Link, BuzzFeed, ProProfs)**
- Catholic-Link's is a Playbuzz embed (observed: https://catholic-link.org/quiz-what-bible-character-are-you/). Beliefnet's is ten questions (search snippet; page 403). Cru's quiz hub (https://www.cru.org/us/en/train-and-grow/quizzes-and-assessments.html) lists nine quizzes, from "How do you really view God?" to "Who's your '90s TV dad?", none gated as far as the hub shows.
- Good: instant, zero vocabulary, a result you can show. This is the owner's own definition of fun.
- Weak: the mapping from answers to a figure is made up. There is nothing to cite. A discernment-minded audience would be right to mock it.
- Spread: Christian radio stations and ministries post them to Facebook (a WAY-FM post appears in the search results).

**11. Saint-match quizzes** — e.g. https://www.saintdiscoveryquiz.com/
- Observed: 21 questions, six traits (contemplative, charitable, intellectual, courageous, joyful, mystical), "480+" saints, no account, no ads visible, operator not named. There are at least eight rivals (3-question and 7-question versions exist), so the category is crowded.
- Good: the six-trait layer is more honest than direct mapping, and a confirmation-saint use case gives it a real seasonal audience.
- Weak: placing 480 saints on six trait scores is, again, one person's invention. CLAUDE.md already kills person-to-saint percentages; nothing here argues for reopening that.

**12. The 5 Love Languages** — https://5lovelanguages.com/
- Who: Gary Chapman / Moody Publishers. Not marketed as Christian, but it is the Christian publishing world's one true mass quiz.
- Claims (observed on the home page): "150+ million people have discovered their Love Language", clarified elsewhere on the same page as "150M+ quizzes taken on this site". Free quiz, plus a $39 premium report, apology-language and anger quizzes, and a "What's Their Love Language" quiz about someone else.
- Good: five categories anyone can name, a result that is about a relationship so it is **always discussed with a second person**, and a quiz you take about somebody else. That last one is a spread mechanic the pair overlay (`/c/`) already points toward.
- Weak: the site warns against "unauthorized" quizzes; the mark is defended. Do not build on it or name anything after it.

**13. Christian Enneagram funnels** — https://www.yourenneagramcoach.com/ and https://www.gospelenneagram.com/assessment
- Observed: free test as the top of a funnel to courses, books and coaching (Your Enneagram Coach, Beth and Jeff McCord); Gospel Enneagram is a ministry programme of "People Launching" selling type courses and coaching. One of these sites itself says any Enneagram test is only about 60 percent accurate (seen in a search summary; not verified on a page).
- Good: warm, type-specific follow-up content keeps people reading for weeks.
- Weak: contested origins inside the church (The Gospel Coalition and others publish "should Christians use it" pieces), email capture, upsell. **Stay out.** It is someone else's framework, it divides the audience, and it cannot be cited to anything a confessional reader accepts.

### Discipleship and spiritual-health assessments

**14. Emotionally Healthy Discipleship personal assessment** — https://www.emotionallyhealthy.org/your-personal-assessment/
- Who: Pete Scazzero's Emotionally Healthy Discipleship.
- Observed: 40 questions, about 15 minutes, **email required**, result is one of four stages: emotional infant, child, teen or adult. A PDF version is offered.
- Good: a blunt, memorable result shape; tied to a book churches run as a course, so whole small groups take it.
- Weak: it ranks a person's maturity from a self-report. Alongside Disciple Labs (40 questions across eight practice categories, sold to churches; site was down when I tried), the Rooted Network "assessment" (a login-gated lead magnet for pastors: https://www.experiencerooted.com/pages/discipleship-assessment) and the fruit-of-the-Spirit self-tests (Resolute's is 27 items and takes name and email first: https://beresolute.org/sfa/), this is the "soul as a stat sheet" genre CLAUDE.md already killed. Nothing I saw argues for reopening it.

### Knowledge tests from research bodies

**15. Pew Research Center, U.S. Religious Knowledge Quiz** — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/quiz/u-s-religious-knowledge-quiz/
- Observed: 15 questions drawn from the 32 asked of 10,971 U.S. adults in February 2019; ungated; at the end you see how you did against the national sample and against groups (by affiliation, attendance, age, education). The report: average 14.2 of 32 correct; Jews, atheists and agnostics scored highest; evangelical Protestants 15.5; 51% knew Jesus gave the Sermon on the Mount; only 20% knew Protestantism traditionally teaches salvation by faith alone (https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/07/23/what-americans-know-about-religion/).
- Good: **this is the best-made quiz in the whole survey.** Every question has a right answer, and the result is a comparison with real people from a real sample. It is the model for "count knowledge, never devotion".
- Weak: it is seven years old, general religion rather than Bible, and static.
- Related: the Ligonier / Lifeway Research "State of Theology" (2025 U.S. wave; a 2025 Canadian wave of 3,000-plus people on 35 statements, published May 2026: https://news.lifeway.com/2026/05/26/canadians-see-split-between-religious-beliefs-and-truth-state-of-theology-finds/). It has a data explorer at thestateoftheology.com (the page returned only a title to my tool, so **I could not verify whether a visitor can answer the statements themselves**). Its framing is one tradition's view of what counts as a wrong answer, and it has been criticised for that (https://goodfaithmedia.org/ligoniers-state-of-theology-survey-alarms-orthodox-gatekeepers/ — headline read, article not opened).

## 3. Patterns across the field

- **Email is the business model.** GiftsTest, Denison, EHD, Resolute, the Enneagram sites and Rooted all take an address. The ungated ones (MinTools, Pew, North Point, Saint Discovery, TheoCompass) are either hobby projects or institutions with other income. Ungated plus beautiful plus cited is a genuinely empty square.
- **The pastor is the distributor.** Gifts tests spread because a pastor or volunteer coordinator sends the link to a class. The features that serve that person are: a printable version, a youth version, a way to see a group's results together without accounts. The site's pair overlay and planned group spread are the right primitives; nobody else has them without logins.
- **Self-ratings flatter.** Agree/disagree items about one's own virtue or gifting invite the answer people wish were true. North Point's "rate actual experience" instruction and Hirsch's forced-choice pairs are the two honest countermeasures I saw.
- **Nobody cites anything.** Not one quiz I opened linked a gift definition to the verse and the range of readings of that verse, or a denominational position to a confession. This is the site's moat, confirmed.
- **Results are dead ends.** Most end at a bar chart and a book offer. Almost none throw off permanent, indexable pages per result. DenominationDifferences is the exception (comparison pages around a quiz), and it is the one hobby site that keeps turning up in search.

## 4. Gaps: wanted, and done badly or not at all

1. **A gifts test that is honest about the lists.** Romans 12, 1 Corinthians 12 and Ephesians 4 are different lists doing different jobs; whether the sign gifts continue is a live dispute. Every test silently picks a side. None lets the user pick, the way Sounds Like Scripture lets the player pick a canon.
2. **End-times views.** My search for an eschatology "which view am I" quiz returned explainers (TGC, Bible Study Tools, Wikipedia) and a YouTube video, and no real quiz. People ask this. It is a natural bipolar-or-multi-position module.
3. **Bible translation chooser that is not evangelical-only.** Compelling Truth's 10-question quiz recommends "a faithful, evangelical translation"; BibleStudyTools chooses among five; Babylon Bee has a parody. I found none that includes Catholic and Orthodox Bibles or explains canon and textual basis. It is a decision tool built from facts, not opinions.
4. **A Bible knowledge benchmark with real comparison data.** Pew's is 2019 and general. Nothing lets you compare your Bible knowledge with a published sample.
5. **"Which confession could I actually sign?"** Denomination quizzes paraphrase. None puts the real sentences of the real documents in front of people.
6. **Something personal that is not a personality typology.** The demand behind Bible-character, saint and Enneagram quizzes is "tell me something about me I can show someone". Every supplier meets it with invented mappings.
7. **Two-person and group modes without accounts.** Love Languages proves relational quizzes travel; church tests prove groups want pooled results; everyone requires a login for it.

## 5. Ten quiz concepts for wiserwalk.com

Each is scored honestly on **authored-content burden** — how many sentences someone has to write that could turn out loaded. The owner has been burned there twice, so the list is ordered with the lowest-burden, highest-confidence ideas first. Before building any of them: put six real items in front of the owner, no machinery.

### 1. Could You Sign It? (the confessions, in their own words)
A card shows one real sentence from a real historic document — the Nicene Creed, the Augsburg Confession, the Thirty-Nine Articles, Westminster, the 1689 Baptist Confession, the Canons of Trent, the Confession of Dositheus. Tap "I'd sign that", "I would not", or "not sure". At the end: which documents you could sign whole, and the exact lines where you part company with each.
- **Burden: near zero authored items.** Every line is verbatim from a public-domain text, provable by the same substring verifier Sounds Like Scripture uses. The work is selection and excerpt length, not writing. (Modern documents such as the Catechism of the Catholic Church and the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 are in copyright: link to them, do not excerpt beyond a phrase.)
- Self-perpetuates: the result is personal and surprising ("I can sign more of Trent than I expected"), and the pair overlay works on it immediately.
- Discoverable: a permanent page per document and per article ("What does Article 17 of the Thirty-Nine Articles say?"), which people do search.
- Risk: archaic language. Sounds Like Scripture already shows the audience tolerates it.

### 2. A Psalm for This (pick up to four cards)
Sixteen plain feeling-words and situations on cards ("worn out", "betrayed", "grateful and I can't say why"). Tap up to four. The result is a Psalm, shown in full from the bundled BSB, with one line on what kind of Psalm it is.
- **Burden: low.** The cards are single words or short phrases, not claims, so they cannot be loaded. The mapping rests on the standard scholarly classification of the Psalms (lament, thanksgiving, trust, royal, wisdom — Gunkel's types, citeable to any standard introduction) and on what each Psalm says about its own occasion in its superscription or first lines. Where a Psalm's type is disputed, leave it out.
- It meets the owner's own definition of fun: personal, no church vocabulary, tactile, a result worth showing someone. It counts nothing.
- Discoverable: permanent pages of the form "a Psalm for when you are anxious / grieving / angry". I believe these are heavily searched but **did not verify volumes**; check before building.
- Risk: drifting into advice. Keep to "here is a Psalm prayed from that place", never "this will fix it".

### 3. Bible Knowledge, Benchmarked
Fifteen right-or-wrong questions; after each, the share of a real published sample that got it right, with the source. Start with the Bible and Christianity questions in Pew's 2019 survey, whose per-question results are published, then add questions only where a published survey figure exists.
- **Burden: low, and factual.** No opinions. The burden is permissions and sourcing: Pew's terms for reusing question wording with attribution need reading first (**not verified**).
- Self-perpetuates: a score against the country is the most shared quiz result there is. Discoverable: one page per question ("Who preached the Sermon on the Mount? 51% of Americans knew").
- This uses the engine's planned right/wrong strategy and fits "count knowledge, never devotion" exactly.

### 4. Which Bible Should I Read? (all three canons)
Six to eight questions of fact about the reader (first Bible or tenth; reading aloud or studying; which church, if any; comfort with older English). Output: two or three translations with reasons, including Catholic and Orthodox options, and a plain account of each one's translation approach, source texts and canon.
- **Burden: medium, but it is data entry, not item writing.** Every attribute comes from the publishers' own prefaces. Nothing is a belief statement, so nothing can be loaded. The risk is fairness between translations; describe each in its translators' stated aims.
- Discoverable: strongest of the ten. "ESV vs NIV", "best Catholic Bible translation" and similar are evergreen questions, and the existing compare-page pattern produces those pages.
- Self-perpetuates: modestly; it is useful rather than fun. People send it to new believers.

### 5. Spiritual gifts, with the list as the player's choice (already in progress)
Keep building it, with four differences from the field. (a) The user chooses the frame first — Romans 12 only, Paul's three lists without the sign gifts, or all of them — and every choice is described in its holders' words, exactly like the canon choice in the game. (b) Items ask what has actually happened ("people have asked me to explain things to them"), never what you feel drawn to. (c) Thirty to forty items at most, or forced-choice pairs. (d) The result says in one sentence what a self-rating cannot tell you, and that every tradition sends you to other people to confirm a gift.
- **Burden: high.** This is the most item-writing of any concept here and the category where loaded items live. Budget an adversarial item review like the Compass audit, at smaller scale.
- Discoverable: one page per gift with every passage and the range of readings. Self-perpetuates through pastors: add a printable version and an accountless group spread.

### 6. The End-Times Map
A small Compass-style module: millennium (pre, post, a), the timing of the rapture where that applies, how Revelation is read (preterist, historicist, futurist, idealist), Israel and the church.
- **Burden: medium-high.** It needs fair statements for every position in its holders' words and an audit. The positions are, however, unusually well-defined and well-documented (position papers from the Assemblies of God, Reformed confessions, standard four-views books), so citation is easy.
- Gap confirmed: no real quiz surfaced in search. Self-perpetuates in exactly the discernment audience that already argues about this. Discoverable: one page per view and compare pages.
- Note for the engine: some of these are multi-position, not bipolar. Do not force them onto two-pole rails.

### 7. Where Would You Have Stood?
Real church-history disputes as decisions: the date of Easter, readmitting the lapsed after persecution, images in churches, the filioque, indulgences. Each card gives the situation and two or three positions in short quotations from the people who held them. You choose; then you see who said it, what happened, and where the churches stand today.
- **Burden: medium.** The situations must be written, but the options are quotations, which is what failed to be true of the rejected figures deck (options written backwards from a citation). Needs about 25 disputes with clean primary sources before launch.
- Personal result: "you sided with X four times" — a named strand of the tradition, not a percentage. Discoverable: a permanent page per dispute. Overlaps with the second game (Who Said It?), so decide whether this is a mode of that game rather than a new quiz.

### 8. A front door for the Compass: "What denomination am I?"
Not a new quiz. A landing page and result framing that meets the phrase people type, routes into the Compass, and then shows the nearest traditions with the existing scope note. Add "take it for someone else / compare with someone" prominently, borrowing the Love Languages move.
- **Burden: very low** (one page of copy). Discoverable by definition. It also answers TheoCompass directly: fewer traditions, all cited, a public disputes log.
- Keep the binding rules: no compatibility percentage between people; hedge poor fits.

### 9. How Christians Have Prayed (a preferences map, not a score)
Cards of concrete practices from across the traditions — a fixed daily office, the Jesus Prayer, extemporaneous prayer, praying the Psalms, lectio divina, silence, singing, the rosary. Tap the ones that draw you; the result describes the two or three historic streams they belong to, each in its own words and sources, with what to read next.
- **Burden: medium.** The cards are names of practices, not claims, so they are hard to load. The prose about each stream needs care and sources. Do not reuse Gary Thomas's nine "sacred pathways": that framework is his, and the free versions online are adaptations of it (Soul Shepherding says so explicitly).
- Hard line: it maps attraction, it never counts or tracks practice. Anything practice-shaped ends with "bring this to your pastor or priest", per the Orthodox positioning rule.
- Discoverable: one page per practice ("What is the Jesus Prayer?").

### 10. Seven deadly sins, by forced choice (finish the existing draft)
Replace "how often do you…" self-ratings with pairs: two temptations, which is louder for you? The structure underneath comes from the tradition itself — Evagrius's eight thoughts, Gregory's seven, and Aquinas's "daughters" of each capital vice (Summa II-II), which give citeable, concrete sub-forms.
- **Burden: high, and the highest risk of the ten.** Every pair is authored, and this is precisely the kind of survey writing that was rejected before. It stays a draft and out of every shared surface until audited. It is on this list because it is already half built and because pairs are the one format in this field that resists flattering self-report.
- Self-perpetuates: strongly, if it is good. Discoverable: one page per vice with the classical sources.

### One that is deliberately not on the list
**"Who in the Bible are you most like?"** is in progress, so I have not re-proposed it; but the research bears on it. Every existing version is an arbitrary mapping, and the honest versions tried so far were rejected on play. The lowest-risk honest shape I can see is the one Concept 2 uses: the user picks from plain cards, and the result is a *text* that speaks from that place, rather than a verdict that the user *is* someone. If the figure quiz goes ahead, show the recorded act and the verse for every point of resemblance, and never a percentage.
