# Gifts quiz, all gifts in: the plain-language critique

**Who is reading.** I have been to a church four times. I know the words church, pray, Bible and
serve. I have never heard charism, continuationist or cessationist, I have never seen anyone speak
in tongues, and I do not know what "interpret" means in a church. I meet these statements one at a
time on a phone, shuffled among fifty-seven, with no headings and no gift names. If a statement
makes me feel stupid or reads like a trick, I pick the middle and move on, or I close the tab.

**What I read.** `gifts-all-statements-A.md` and `gifts-all-statements-B.md` in full, the quiz as
it stands (`spiritual-gifts.ts`), `gifts-final.md`, the blueprint's six rules, the audit log's
decisions 1, 4 and 6, the Pentecostal and cessationist audits, and the Compass's Gifts summary.
Nothing under `site/` was touched and nothing was committed.

**What I counted.** Every candidate in both drafts was word-counted by script. Both drafters'
counts are right on every line. Nothing is over 21 words; the longest is A's P4 at 21. Every
rewrite I offer below was counted the same way, and the 16 Scripture quotations I reuse in the
summaries were run through `webbe.mjs --check` (16 of 16 verbatim).

**How the score moves, because several findings depend on it.** Three statements per gift, each
answer worth -2 to +2, the reverse one flipped. A row is 50 plus 8.3 for each net point. Below 50
prints "not found here", 58 prints "a little", 67 and 75 print "some of this", and a gift can be
named from 75.

---

## The verdict in one paragraph

Both drafts did the hard part well: nothing counts praying, nothing says in the site's voice that
an event was a miracle or a word from God, and nearly every sentence is short. B writes the
plainer sentence for the disagreement and the better reverse items for healing and prophecy. A
writes the plainer tongues and interpretation statements and catches the newcomer problem on
reverse items more often. What goes wrong is small and fixable: **reverse items with two halves
that a newcomer must half-deny**, **one jargon sentence repeated on six pages**, **four garden-path
sentences**, **four phrases that point at nothing**, **a Latin-prayer reader who can score on
tongues by accident**, and **one statement everybody on earth agrees with**. The final set has to
be mixed from both.

---

## Eight things that go wrong (read before the tables)

### 1. A reverse item with two halves, or with a premise I have never met, raises a gift I do not have

This is the most damaging fault, and both drafters half-saw it. A calls it the chair-two problem;
B calls it the hole in a conditional. B then offers a shape it says has no hole ("something I have
heard others do, not done myself") and that shape has a different hole: it is two claims, and I can
truthfully deny the first half.

- B T5: "Praying in words of no known language is something I have heard others do, not done
  myself." I have never heard anyone do it. Half of this is false for me, so I pick Disagree.
  Disagreeing with a reverse item RAISES tongues.
- B I5: "Explaining words spoken in an unknown language is something I have seen others do, not
  done myself." Same: I have never seen it. Disagree. Interpretation goes up.
- A H4: "When someone is ill, I pray for them on my own rather than with them." I do neither.
  Disagree. Healing goes up. (A saw this and preferred H5.)
- A T5, A I4, A M5, B I4, B H5, B M5, B P4: all open with a "When" I have never been in.

What it does to the score. If I answer the two forward statements "Strongly disagree" the harm is
nil: -4 plus 2 is -2, the row reads 33, "not found here". The harm comes when the forward
statements confuse me and I pick "Unsure / neither" on both, then disagree with the reverse because
its premise is false: 0, 0 and +2 make a row of 67, and a person who has never heard of tongues is
shown "tongues: some of this". So confusing forward statements and a holed reverse are one fault, not
two, and the six new gifts are where forward statements are most likely to confuse.

Two rules fall out of it, and I have applied them to every recommendation:

- **On a reverse item, the honest answer of someone who has never been there must be Agree.**
  "Every word I have ever prayed has been in a language I know" passes even for someone who has
  never prayed. "When someone speaks in a language nobody there has learnt, I have no idea what it
  means" passes because "I would have no idea" is the natural answer whether or not it has ever
  happened. "I would rather bring them a meal than pray with them" passes.
- **"If" is safer than "When" on a reverse item.** "When" says it happens; "If" invites me to
  imagine it. A's P4 ("If I believe God has given me something...") can be answered by someone it
  has never happened to. The same sentence opening with "When" could not.

A conditional on a FORWARD statement is harmless: I disagree, and the gift goes down, which is true.

### 2. The reader who prays in Latin scores on tongues

A T1: "I have prayed aloud in a language I have never learnt." A says "never learnt" keeps out the
reader who recites prayers in Latin or Church Slavonic, "mostly". It does not. Someone who has
memorised the Pater Noster has not learnt Latin, and will truthfully agree. The same reader meets
A T4, "Every word I have ever prayed has been in a language I know", and truthfully disagrees. That
is +2 and +2 on two of three statements: a Catholic or Orthodox reader who has never spoken in
tongues is shown "tongues: some of this". B saw half of this (it refused "words I do not
understand" for the same reason) but B T1 and B T4 rest on "a language I have learnt" and have the
same gap, plus a second one: a literal-minded teenager did not "learn" English, they just speak it.

Fix on the forward statement, where it matters: **"I have prayed in a language I never learnt, in
words nobody taught me."** (14). "Words nobody taught me" shuts out a memorised prayer in any
language. With that fixed, T4 can stay as A wrote it: the Latin reader now scores -2, -2, +2, which
is 33 and "not found here".

### 3. Four garden paths: sentences where I trip and have to start again

- A M1: "People who were there have called something that happened when I prayed a miracle." The
  eye reads "when I prayed a miracle". The word that finishes "called" arrives seven words late.
- B P3: "People have come back to tell me that something I said was from God proved true." Two
  verbs stacked: I read "something I said was from God", full stop, and then hit "proved true".
- B T3: "Something I said in church in words I did not know was then explained by someone else."
  "in church in words", and "words I did not know" sounds like long words.
- B I2: "I have said in plain words what someone had just said in a language neither of us knew."
  "said ... what someone had just said" makes me read it twice, and "neither of us" means there
  were only two people in the room.

### 4. Four phrases that point at nothing inside the sentence

- A's disagreement sentence, on all six pages: "passed away once **that foundation** was laid."
  What foundation? The Compass says "the apostolic foundation"; A changed it to "that" and the
  word it pointed to is gone.
- B F1: "People have told me they kept going because I was sure **it** would come right." What
  would? The first edit threw out B faith-2 for exactly this.
- B M1: "could not be explained **any other way**." Other than what? The sentence never says.
- A Ea: "People who do not share my beliefs ask me about **them**." The beliefs, or the people?
  Small, but B E1 has no such wobble.

Also A's suggested intro line, "nobody could write **it** otherwise": write what?

word" and "the laying on of hands" in the site's own voice (B healing); "he has it weighed" (B

consolation" (a quotation, so it stays, but it needs one plain clause after it).

### 6. One statement everybody agrees with, and one that reads as a "should"

- A K1: "People have told me that a fact I gave them was exactly what they needed to know." I told
  my friend which bus to catch and she said "that’s exactly what I needed". I agree, strongly. So
  does everyone who has ever given directions. Then the result shows me "the word of knowledge",
  and its page tells me Christians dispute whether that gift is given today. Perfectly plain, and
  it measures nothing. "Exactly" is also an intensifier fishing for a yes.
- B H3: "When somebody tells me they are ill, I pray with them there and then." Plain. But I read
  it as something I am supposed to do, and I do not. Agreeing sounds devout and disagreeing sounds
  slack, which is rule 3, and any keen Christian taught to pray on the spot agrees whatever their
  gift, which is rule 6 by the back door. B's own spare ("More than once, someone I prayed with has
  recovered when nobody expected them to") reports an event and has neither fault.

### 7. The same thing asked twice, which a reader on a phone notices

- B's tongues three (T1, T2, T4) are one fact three times: I pray in a language I never learnt;
  people have heard me do it; all my praying is in a language I learnt. A's set asks a second
  thing (T2: it happened in church and someone said what it meant), which reads as a new question.
- B K1 with B K5: "people ask me where the Bible says something" and "I am the one asking rather
  than answering" are mirror images. The drafters both removed an evangelism statement for being
  its reverse item turned round; the same rule applies here. Ship one of the pair.
- Tongues' "someone said what it meant after I spoke" and interpretation's "I said what it meant
  after someone spoke" are the same sentence with the people swapped. They must not open with the
  same words or a skimming reader answers one as the other. My rewrites start one with "When I
  have spoken" and the other with "I have given the meaning".
- Prophecy: A P1 ("something I said to them was what God wanted them to hear") beside a one-to-one
  event statement would be one event from two sides. Keeping the event statement in church (A P3)
  makes it a second question.

### 8. "I have ..." facts on an agree-to-disagree scale

Eleven of my eighteen recommended statements for the new gifts are events ("I have prayed...",
"People have told me..."). To "I have prayed in a language I never learnt" my honest answer is
"No, never", and the button says "Strongly disagree", which is an odd way to say no. It works, and
I would get used to it, but two small things would help and cost nothing:

- One line above the scale for this quiz: "Agree means this is true of you. Disagree means it is
  not. If it has never come up, disagree is an honest answer."  Do NOT tell readers to pick
  "Unsure / neither" when they have never been in the situation: finding 1 shows that is the
  answer that lets a holed reverse item name a gift.
- Do not let a newcomer's first three minutes be the strangest statements. B suggested placing
  tongues and interpretation late. The running order takes each gift's first statement in round
  one, so put each new gift's least startling statement in its first slot (the meal, "where the
  Bible says something", "I leave it to others", "things I hear about from others") and the bare
  events in the third, while still keeping the reverse items spread across the rounds.

## Interpreting tongues

Use one phrase for the language in every tongues and interpretation statement: "a language nobody
there had learnt". A reader who has worked it out once should not have to work out "an unknown
language", "words none of us knew" and "a language nobody knows" as well. "Nobody there" includes
me, which is what keeps the person who translates Polish for a visitor from agreeing.

# The one sentence about the disagreement

**A's**, on all six pages: "Many Christians hold that this gift remains available to the church
until Christ returns and is to be welcomed and tested; many hold that it authenticated the apostles
and their message and passed away once that foundation was laid."

As a newcomer I fail on "authenticated", I do not know what "that foundation" is (finding 4), and
at 40 words I have lost the first half by the time I finish the second. It is faithful to the
Compass and it is fair: each side gets its own reason. It is not plain.

**B's**: "Many Christians hold that this gift is given today, to be welcomed and tested; many hold
that it was given for the time of the apostles."

I understand this one. Four things still catch:

1. **It can be read as no disagreement at all.** "Given today" and "given for the time of the
   apostles" are not opposites to someone who does not already know the argument: could it not be
   both? The second half needs "only". B uses "only" in its wisdom and faith sentence and leaves it
   out here.
2. **"To be welcomed and tested" sounds like the site giving advice.** The clause has no "they
   say" of its own, so I hear the page telling me what to do with the gift. It also gives the first
   side a warm extra clause and the second side none. Either both sides get a reason or neither
   does. The brief's own example gives neither, and that is the plainer choice; the reasons live on
   the Compass page the next line links to.
3. "Hold" is a church-paper word. "Believe" is what I would say.
4. "Is given": by whom? Say it.

**Recommended, for the six added gifts** (21 words):

> Many Christians believe God still gives this gift today; many believe he gave it only for the
> time of the apostles.

Tested for side-taking: "many" and "many", never "most" or "some"; the same verb for both; neither
side named; neither given a reason; "still" assumes only that the gift was once given, which both
hold; the order follows the Compass's own. Healing adds B's clause as its own sentence: **"Both
believe God still heals in answer to prayer."** The existing link line then follows once per page.

One word I cannot fix: "apostles". After four visits I may not know it. It is the word both sides
use and any gloss would be the site's own, so I would leave it and let the Compass page carry it.

**For wisdom, faith and discernment.** A's version is 31 words and asks me to find "this sentence
of Paul’s". B's is better. Plainer still, and with "only" (21 words):

> Christians who believe some gifts were only for the time of the apostles disagree about whether
> this is one of them.

Both drafters say this sentence is uncited and must stay this general unless someone opens the
books. It reads clearly on its own, without having seen the six-gift sentence first.

# The summaries, as I would hand them to a fourteen-year-old

Built from the drafters' own sentences; every quotation below was re-checked verbatim. [S6] is the
six-gift sentence above and [S3] the wisdom, faith and discernment sentence.

- **The word of knowledge.** The passage names a word of knowledge, something said, and nowhere
  explains it. Some Christians take it as supplying what God has made known at the moment it is
  needed, others as being given a fact that nobody had told you, and these statements ask about
  both. [S6] *(See my note above on "what God has made known".)*
- **Healing.** The translation quoted here says gifts of healings, with both words in the plural,
  and Paul asks, “Do all have gifts of healings?” (1 Corinthians 12:30). These statements ask what
  has happened when you prayed for people who were ill and what they said afterwards. They cannot
  tell this gift apart from an answer to prayer, and nothing here counts how much anyone prays.
  [S6] Both believe God still heals in answer to prayer.
- **Miracles.** The translation quoted here says workings of miracles, and later “miracle workers”
  (1 Corinthians 12:28). Luke writes that “God worked special miracles by the hands of Paul” (Acts
  19:11): the doing is God’s. These statements ask what has happened when you prayed and what the
  people who were there called it. This page calls nothing a miracle itself. [S6]
- **Prophecy.** Paul says what it is for: “he who prophesies speaks to men for their edification,
  exhortation, and consolation” (1 Corinthians 14:3). He also tells the church to weigh it: “let
  the others discern” (1 Corinthians 14:29). These statements ask whether you have said what you
  believed God gave you to say, and what people said afterwards. Whether any of it was from God is
  for your church to weigh, not this page. [S6] *(Editor to decide on a plain clause after the
  first quotation.)*
- **Tongues.** The translation quoted here says different kinds of languages where many others say
  tongues: speaking or praying in a language the speaker has never learnt. At Pentecost “everyone
  heard them speaking in his own language” (Acts 2:6); to Corinth Paul writes of a speaker whom
  “no one understands” (1 Corinthians 14:2). These statements ask only whether this has happened
  to you. [S6]
- **Interpreting tongues.** The translation quoted here says the interpretation of languages:
  giving a church the meaning of what was said in a language it does not know, so that, in Paul’s
  words, “the assembly may be built up” (1 Corinthians 14:5). Acts never shows anyone doing it;
  what the New Testament has is Paul’s instructions to Corinth, below. [S6]
- **Discernment.** The passage says discerning of spirits: telling what is behind a message, a
  teaching or a claim to speak for God. Paul tells the church to weigh what prophets say, “let the
  others discern” (1 Corinthians 14:29), and John writes, “test the spirits, whether they are of
  God” (1 John 4:1). These statements ask what has happened when you weighed such things. They say
  nothing about how you knew, and they are not about first impressions of people. [S3]
- **Wisdom and faith.** As they stand, plus [S3]. B is right to drop "everyday" from wisdom's
  summary only if the editor thinks it now reads as a slight beside the six new pages; from my
  chair "the everyday pattern" is the clearest phrase in it.

**Frame copy the change forces.** B's intro concession is the clearer of the two; A's ends on a
dangling "it". Plainer still: "With a few gifts, such as tongues and healing, there is no honest
way to ask without showing which gift is meant." A's catch in the result note is a good one and
reads naturally: "Some of the everyday patterns here are temperament as much as gift." B's
not-scored sentence is plain as written.
