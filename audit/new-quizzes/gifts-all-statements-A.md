# What are your spiritual gifts? All gifts in: Drafter A

Angle: **start from the reader.** For each gift I sat in two chairs. Chair one: a person who has
this, in a church that welcomes it. What could they truthfully say has happened? Chair two: a
person who has never seen it, or whose church holds it was for the time of the apostles. What
would they truthfully say? A statement is kept only if both can answer it without lying, without
being insulted and without being cornered.

Nothing under `site/` was touched. Nothing was committed. I did not read Drafter B's file.

Every verse quoted or described below was opened with `node audit/tools/webbe.mjs` (World English
Bible British Edition). The 36 quotations in this file were run through `webbe.mjs --check` and all
36 are verbatim substrings of the verses named. Every word count below was counted by script, not
by eye. Longest candidate: 21 words (P4).

Direction: **+1** agreeing raises the gift, **-1** agreeing lowers it.
"Others" means the statement reports what other people bring to the reader or have said of them.

## The three things I would want the editor to know before reading the tables

1. **A gift that is a fact will beat every gift that is a pattern.** The Pentecostal reviewer was
   right that whether someone has prayed in a language they never learnt is a fact. Three fact
   statements would put every such reader at 100 on that row while their pattern rows sit at 67 to
   83, so the headline would read "tongues" for every reader who has ever done it, whatever else is
   true of them. My answer is in the candidates: for tongues, healing, miracles and interpretation
   the recommended three are built so that the bare fact earns about two thirds and the top of the
   row needs the part 1 Corinthians 12:7 is about, "for the profit of all": it happened in the
   church, and other people said something about it. 1 Corinthians 14:28 itself separates the two
   ("let him keep silent in the assembly, and let him speak to himself and to God").
2. **For every reverse item I asked what the chair-two reader answers.** A reverse item that a
   reader with no experience is pushed to DISAGREE with raises a gift they do not have (the first
   edit caught this once, on wisdom). Each reverse candidate below says what that reader answers.
3. **Rule 5 is lost for tongues, interpretation, healing and miracles, and mostly lost for
   prophecy.** Nobody can write "I have prayed in a language I never learnt" so that a reader
   cannot tell what it feeds. I have not tried to disguise them, because a disguised statement
   about these gifts turns into a feeling or a hunch, which is the other thing forbidden. The intro
   concession ("you will sometimes see which gift a statement is about") should lose "sometimes"
   or gain a second sentence; suggested wording is in the notes at the end.

# JOB 1. The six added gifts

## 1. The word of knowledge (`knowledge`)

**Display name:** the word of knowledge. **Slug:** `knowledge`.

**The passage it is named from (verbatim):**
- 1 Corinthians 12:8: “and to another the word of knowledge according to the same Spirit”

**The two chairs.** The passage gives the name and no explanation, and I found two honest chair-one
readers, not one. (a) In churches that welcome it, the usual account is: I knew something about a
person or a situation that nobody had told me, I said it, and it was true. (b) In many other
churches the reader is the person who knows what the Bible says and can put their hand on it when
it is needed. Chair two says: what I know, somebody told me or I looked up. Both (a) and (b) can be
reported as events, silent on how the knowing happened. I have not attributed (a) or (b) to named
traditions in the summary, because I did not open a confession or standard work that says so, and
the rule is nothing uncited.

| id | statement | dir | words | type | the risk I see |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | People have told me that a fact I gave them was exactly what they needed to know. | +1 | 17 | others | Works for both readings and is silent on how. Sits close to prophecy P1 ("what God wanted them to hear"): fact here, message there. If both ship, expect the two rows to rise together. |
| K2 | People have asked me how I knew something about them that they had never told me. | +1 | 16 | others | Reading (a) only. A perceptive reader, or one who hears gossip, agrees truthfully. Reports what people asked, asserts nothing about the source. |
| K3 | I have known something about a person’s situation that nobody had told me, and it proved true. | +1 | 17 | event | Reading (a) only. "Known" followed by "proved true" is the reader's own judgement of their record; memory keeps the hits. Near 1 Corinthians 14:25 (“the secrets of his heart are revealed”), which Paul writes of prophecy, so a Pentecostal editor may say this belongs to P. |
| K4 | When a question about the Bible comes up, I would rather look it up than answer from memory. | -1 | 18 | reverse | Reading (b) only. A real trade-off: looking it up is the careful habit, so nobody is shamed. Chair two agrees, which is the right way round. Says nothing to reading (a). |
| K5 | What I know about a person’s situation is what they or someone else has told me. | -1 | 16 | reverse | Reading (a) only, and it is K3 turned inside out, so K3 and K5 together ask one thing twice. Will sound odd ("well, obviously") to a newcomer; they will agree, which lowers a row they do not have, so no harm. |

Spare, because reading (b) has no "others" candidate above: "People ask me where the Bible says
something, and I can usually tell them." (+1, 14 words, others). Counts knowledge, not reading
hours, so rule 6 holds. Risk: close to teaching's "People come to me to have something explained";
the difference is supplying the passage against making it understood.

**My three:** K1, K3, K4. **Said plainly: three statements cannot serve both readings equally.**
With K1 + K3 + K4 a reading-(a) reader can reach "a lot of this" and a reading-(b) reader tops out
at "some of this". Swap K3 for the spare and it reverses. I chose (a) for the second forward slot
because the owner's decision is about not leaving out these gifts as the churches that practise
them know them, and because teaching's row already sees most of reading (b). The editor may choose
the other way; either way the summary below owns the limit.

**Summary for the gift page:**
> The passage names a word of knowledge, something said, and does not explain it, and Christians
> understand it in more than one way. These statements ask what you have known and said and what
> people told you afterwards; they cannot see how you came to know it. Many Christians hold that
> this gift remains available to the church until Christ returns and is to be welcomed and tested;
> many hold that it authenticated the apostles and their message and passed away once that
> foundation was laid. The Theology Compass’s Gifts page sets out both positions in full.

**Recorded in the early church.** The New Testament calls no act a word of knowledge. These are
the places where Paul writes of knowledge as something given to a church and spoken in it:
- **1 Corinthians 1:4-7.** Paul thanks God that the Corinthians were enriched in Christ in all speech and all knowledge, so that they come behind in no gift.
- **1 Corinthians 14:6.** Paul asks what he would profit them by coming with other languages unless he spoke to them by way of revelation, or of knowledge, or of prophesying, or of teaching.
- **Romans 15:14.** Paul writes that he is persuaded the Christians at Rome are filled with all knowledge and able also to admonish others.
- **1 Corinthians 13:2.** Paul writes that if he has the gift of prophecy and knows all mysteries and all knowledge, but does not have love, he is nothing.

Left for the editor: **Acts 5:1-4** sits under discernment today. The cessationist reviewer wanted
it off that page because Pentecostal teaching files it under the word of knowledge and the text
names neither gift. With knowledge now in, a Pentecostal reader will look for it here. The text
does not say how Peter knew. My advice: one page only, and whichever page has it adds "Luke does
not say how Peter knew". I would leave it where it is rather than make a new connection.

## 2. Healing (`healing`)

**Display name:** healing. **Slug:** `healing`.

**The passages it is named from (verbatim):**
- 1 Corinthians 12:9: “and to another gifts of healings by the same Spirit”
- 1 Corinthians 12:28: “then gifts of healings”
- 1 Corinthians 12:30: “Do all have gifts of healings?”

**The two chairs.** Chair one can truthfully say: I prayed for people who were ill, some of them
got better, they told me so, and now ill people ask for me. Chair two can truthfully say: I pray
for ill friends, on my own, and if they recover I thank God and the doctors; nobody has ever come
back to tell me my praying did it. Note that chair two here includes cessationists who, in the
Compass's words, "affirm that God still heals and answers prayer". So no statement below asks
whether God heals. Each asks what happened around the reader and what people said.

| id | statement | dir | words | type | the risk I see |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

"After", not "because": the statement does not say the prayer did it. A reader in a church that says this readily will hear it more often than one in a church that never would; that is culture, not gift, and no wording removes it. |
| H2 | People who are ill ask me, in particular, to pray for them. | +1 | 12 | others | The Pentecostal reviewer's wording. Counts other people's requests, not the reader's praying, so rule 6 holds narrowly. But in any church the person known for praying is asked, so it partly measures a reputation for devotion, and "in particular" flatters. |
| H3 | More than once, someone I prayed with has recovered when their doctors did not expect it. | +1 | 16 | event | Reports an event and asserts nothing about its cause. A cessationist to whom this has happened will agree truthfully and see "a little" on a row they hold has ceased; the summary must carry "cannot tell a gift from an answered prayer" for that reader. "Their doctors did not expect it" is second-hand medicine. |
| H4 | When someone is ill, I pray for them on my own rather than with them. | -1 | 15 | reverse | Both ends honourable: Matthew 6:6 tells the reader to pray in the inner room with the door shut, so agreeing is not shameful. | H5 | When someone wants prayer for an illness, I point them to somebody else. | -1 | 13 | reverse | Agreeing is modest, not shameful; disagreeing is somebody's real practice. Not written, on purpose: anything that sets prayer against medicine ("my first thought is which
treatment would help"). It would key going to the doctor as the absence of a gift, which insults
Pentecostal readers, who do both.

**My three:** H1 (others), H3 (event), H5 (reverse). A reader for whom one recovery followed one
prayer lands mid-row; the top needs "more than once" and people saying so.

**Summary for the gift page:**
> The translation quoted here says gifts of healings, both words in the plural, and Paul asks, “Do
> all have gifts of healings?” (1 Corinthians 12:30). These statements ask what has happened when
> you prayed for people who were ill and what they said afterwards; they cannot tell a gift from an
> answered prayer, and nothing here counts how much anyone prays. Many Christians hold that this
> gift remains available to the church until Christ returns and is to be welcomed and tested; many
> hold that it authenticated the apostles and their message and passed away once that foundation
> was laid, while affirming that God still heals and answers prayer. The Theology Compass’s Gifts
> page sets out both positions in full.

**Recorded in the early church:**
- **Acts 3:1-8.** At the temple gate Peter told a man lame from birth that he had no silver or gold but would give what he had, and said in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk; he took him by the right hand and raised him up, immediately his feet and ankle bones received strength, and he went into the temple walking, leaping and praising God.
- **Acts 9:32-35.** At Lydda Peter found Aeneas, paralysed and bedridden for eight years, told him that Jesus Christ heals him and to get up and make his bed; immediately he arose, and all who lived at Lydda and in Sharon saw him and turned to the Lord.
- **Acts 28:8-9.** On Malta the father of Publius lay sick with fever and dysentery; Paul went in to him, prayed, and laying his hands on him healed him, and then the rest on the island who had diseases came and were cured.
- **James 5:14-15** (optional fourth; it names elders and prayer, not a gift). James tells anyone who is sick to call for the elders of the church, who are to pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord, and says the prayer of faith will heal him who is sick and the Lord will raise him up.

Spare if James is not wanted: **Acts 14:8-10.** At Lystra Paul, seeing that a man crippled from
birth had faith to be made whole, said with a loud voice, stand upright on your feet; he leapt up
and walked. (The first edit refused this verse for the gift of faith because the faith Luke
mentions is the lame man's. As an act of healing it has no such problem.)

## 3. Miracles (`miracles`)

**Display name:** miracles. **Slug:** `miracles`.

**The passages it is named from (verbatim):**
- 1 Corinthians 12:10: “and to another workings of miracles”
- 1 Corinthians 12:28: “then miracle workers”
- 1 Corinthians 12:29: “Are all miracle workers?”

**The two chairs.** This is the hardest chair one to sit in, and the Pentecostal reviewer said
nobody should score themselves on it. The honest version is not "I work miracles". It is: something
happened when I prayed, the people who were there called it a miracle, and some of them believe
because of it (the pattern of Acts 9:42 and 13:12, where what people saw is followed by belief).
Chair two says: everything I have seen God do came through ordinary means. That sentence is the
cessationist position in the Compass's own vocabulary ("the Spirit's ordinary work"), so it can be
a reverse item that is somebody's creed, not somebody's shame.

> The translation quoted here says workings of miracles, and in 1 Corinthians 12:28 miracle
> workers. These statements ask what has happened when you prayed and what the people who were

> remains available to the church until Christ returns and is to be welcomed and tested; many hold
> that it authenticated the apostles and their message and passed away once that foundation was
> laid. The Theology Compass’s Gifts page sets out both positions in full.

**Recorded in the early church** (the last two are one verse for each reading, as discernment has):
- **Acts 19:11-12.** Luke writes that God worked special miracles by the hands of Paul, so that even handkerchiefs or aprons were carried from his body to the sick, and the diseases departed from them and the evil spirits went out.
- **Acts 9:40-42.** At Joppa Peter sent them all out, knelt down and prayed, and turning to the body told Tabitha to get up; she opened her eyes and sat up, he presented her alive to the saints and widows, and it became known throughout Joppa and many believed in the Lord.
- **Galatians 3:5.** Paul asks the Galatians whether he who supplies the Spirit to them and does miracles amongst them does it by the works of the law or by hearing of faith.
- **2 Corinthians 12:12.** Paul writes to Corinth that the signs of an apostle were worked amongst them in all perseverance, in signs and wonders and mighty works.

Not used, to avoid citing one event on two pages: Acts 13:11-12 (Elymas; 13:8-10 is discernment's),
Acts 8:6-7 (Philip; evangelism's). Acts 9:36-39 is mercy's and 9:40-42 continues the same story;
that seems right to me, since it is two acts by two people, but the editor should see it.

## 4. Prophecy (`prophecy`)

**Display name:** prophecy. **Slug:** `prophecy`.

**The passages it is named from (verbatim):**
- 1 Corinthians 12:10: “and to another prophecy”
- Romans 12:6: “if prophecy, let’s prophesy according to the proportion of our faith”

**The two chairs.** Chair one can truthfully say: I believed God had given me something to say, I
said it to a person or to the church, and people told me afterwards it was for them. The honest
wording keeps "I believed" inside the reader's mouth, so the site never says it WAS from God, and a
Pentecostal is not told their gift is a hunch: it is described exactly as they would describe it.
Chair two says: that has never happened to me, and if it did I would keep quiet. A third honest
reader exists, the preacher who is told "God spoke to me through your sermon"; P1 lets that reader
answer truthfully too without the page deciding whether preaching is prophecy.

| id | statement | dir | words | type | the risk I see |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | People have told me that something I said to them was what God wanted them to hear. | +1 | 17 | others | Reports other people's words; silent on how; answerable in a Pentecostal church and in a pulpit. Close to K1 (fact) and to encouraging (a push to act); the word "God" is what separates it. Flattering to agree with, as every "others" item is. |
| P2 | People ask me whether God has shown me anything for them. | +1 | 11 | others | A true report of something that happens. But it describes a habit many Pentecostal pastors themselves warn against (treating a person as an oracle), so printing it may read as endorsing it. I would not ship it; it is here so the editor sees the alternative. |
| P3 | I have told a church gathering something I believed God had given me to say. | +1 | 15 | event | Points straight at its own result; rule 5 cannot be met here. A preacher may agree on the ground that every sermon is this. "Church gathering" is the plainest I could find for what the translation calls the assembly. |
| P4 | If I believe God has given me something for another person, I keep it to myself rather than risk being wrong. | -1 | 21 | reverse | 21 words, the longest here, and long on a phone. A real trade-off: 1 Corinthians 14:29-32 expects prophets to be weighed and to keep silent in turn, so holding back is nobody's shame. Chair two has never met the "if" and picks the middle or agrees; neither raises the row. |
| P5 | When I speak to a group, I say only what I have prepared. | -1 | 13 | reverse | Keys preparation as the gift's absence, which a preacher who holds that preaching is prophecy will object to. Also near the figure quiz's Plans ahead / Meets the day axis, and the first edit's rule is that one behaviour cannot be temperament in one quiz and a gift in the other. Most readers never speak to groups and pick the middle. |

**My three:** P1 (others), P3 (event), P4 (reverse).

**Summary for the gift page:**
> Paul describes it in the same letter: “he who prophesies speaks to men for their edification,
> exhortation, and consolation” (1 Corinthians 14:3), and he tells the church to weigh it: “let the
> others discern” (1 Corinthians 14:29). These statements ask what you have said and what people
> said of it afterwards; whether any of it was from God is for your church to weigh, not this page.
> Many Christians hold that this gift remains available to the church until Christ returns and is
> to be welcomed and tested; many hold that it authenticated the apostles and their message and
> passed away once that foundation was laid. The Theology Compass’s Gifts page sets out both
> positions in full.

**Recorded in the early church:**
- **Acts 11:27-30.** Prophets came down from Jerusalem to Antioch; one of them, Agabus, stood up and indicated by the Spirit that there would be a great famine over all the world, which Luke says happened in the days of Claudius, and the disciples determined to send relief to the brothers in Judea.
- **Acts 13:1-3.** In the church at Antioch there were prophets and teachers; as they served the Lord and fasted, the Holy Spirit said to separate Barnabas and Saul for the work to which he had called them, and when they had fasted and prayed and laid their hands on them, they sent them away.
- **Acts 21:8-11.** Philip the evangelist had four virgin daughters who prophesied; while Paul’s company stayed with him, a prophet named Agabus took Paul’s belt, bound his own feet and hands, and said that the Holy Spirit says the Jews at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns the belt in this way and deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles.
- **1 Corinthians 14:29-32.** Paul tells Corinth to let two or three of the prophets speak and the others discern, to let the first keep silent if a revelation is made to another sitting by, and says that the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets.

Acts 15:30-32 (Judas and Silas, "being prophets themselves") stays on the encouraging page, where
the audit put the phrase back. A cross-link would serve better than citing it twice.

One boundary to state on the page or in the not-scored sentence: **prophecy is scored; whether
anybody is a prophet is not**, exactly as shepherding's summary says a result "says nothing about
who should hold an office". 1 Corinthians 12:28 and Ephesians 4:11 name prophets as people given
to the church.

## 5. Tongues (`tongues`)

**Display name:** tongues. **Slug:** `tongues`.

**The passages it is named from (verbatim):**
- 1 Corinthians 12:10: “to another different kinds of languages”
- 1 Corinthians 12:28: “and various kinds of languages”
- 1 Corinthians 12:30: “Do all speak with various languages?”

**The two chairs.** Chair one: I have prayed aloud in a language I never learnt; in church I have
done it and someone gave the meaning. Chair two: every word I have ever prayed was in a language I
know. Both are plain facts and neither is an insult. The design problem is the one in note 1 at the
top: a fact asked three times is still one fact. So the three I recommend ask three different
things: has it happened at all (T1), has it happened in the church with its meaning given, which
is the form 1 Corinthians 14:27 asks for (T2), and the chair-two creed (T4).

| T2 | Someone has given the meaning after I spoke aloud in a language nobody there had learnt. | +1 | 16 | others | The "others" here is another person's act, not their opinion. > church until Christ returns and is to be welcomed and tested; many hold that it authenticated the

**Recorded in the early church:**
- **Acts 2:4-13.** At Pentecost they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak with other languages as the Spirit gave them the ability; Jews from every nation came together bewildered, because everyone heard them speaking in his own language the mighty works of God, and others, mocking, said they were filled with new wine.
- **Acts 10:44-46.** While Peter was still speaking, the Holy Spirit fell on all who heard the word, and the believers who had come with Peter were amazed that the gift of the Holy Spirit was poured out on the Gentiles also, for they heard them speaking in other languages and magnifying God.
- **Acts 19:6-7.** When Paul laid his hands on about twelve disciples at Ephesus, the Holy Spirit came on them and they spoke with other languages and prophesied.
- **1 Corinthians 14:18-19.** Paul thanks God that he speaks with other languages more than all of them, and says that in the church he would rather speak five words with his understanding, to instruct others, than ten thousand words in another language.

## 6. Interpreting tongues (`interpretation`)

**Display name:** interpreting tongues. **Slug:** `interpretation`.

**The passages it is named from (verbatim):**
- 1 Corinthians 12:10: “and to another the interpretation of languages”
- 1 Corinthians 12:30: “Do all interpret?”
- 1 Corinthians 14:13: “let him who speaks in another language pray that he may interpret”
- 1 Corinthians 14:27-28: “let one interpret. But if there is no interpreter, let him keep silent in the assembly, and let him speak to himself and to God.”

**The two chairs.** Chair one: someone spoke in a language none of us had learnt, I gave the
meaning, and others said it was right. Chair two: I have never been in that room, and if I were I
would have no idea what was meant. Most readers, including most Pentecostal readers, sit in chair
two, so both reverse items had to be answerable by someone who has never heard any of this. A
person who translates Spanish at church cannot agree by accident: every statement says a language
nobody there had learnt, and that includes the reader.

| I5 | When people speak in languages nobody there has learnt, I have no idea what is meant. | -1 | 16 | reverse | Every chair can answer it: the cessationist agrees, the Pentecostal without this gift agrees, only the reader with it disagrees. A reader who has never heard it may hesitate over "when"; agreeing is still the natural answer. |

**My three:** I1 (event), I3 (others), I5 (reverse).

**Summary for the gift page:**
> The translation quoted here says the interpretation of languages: giving a church the meaning of
> what was spoken in a language it does not know, so that, in Paul’s words, “the assembly may be
> built up” (1 Corinthians 14:5). Acts records no instance of it; what the New Testament has is
> Paul’s instructions to Corinth, below. Many Christians hold that this gift remains available to
> the church until Christ returns and is to be welcomed and tested; many hold that it authenticated
> the apostles and their message and passed away once that foundation was laid. The Theology
> Compass’s Gifts page sets out both positions in full.

Note for the verifier: the quoted words “the assembly may be built up” are inside the longer
checked string “unless he interprets, that the assembly may be built up” (1 Corinthians 14:5).

**Recorded in the early church.** I searched Acts for "interpret": the only two hits are Luke
translating names (Acts 4:36, Acts 13:8). Acts records no act of this gift, and the page should
say so in those words. What the letters record is instruction:
- **1 Corinthians 14:5.** Paul writes that he who prophesies is greater than he who speaks with other languages, unless he interprets, so that the church may be built up.
- **1 Corinthians 14:13.** Paul tells the one who speaks in another language to pray that he may interpret.
- **1 Corinthians 14:26-28.** Paul writes that when they come together one has another language and one has an interpretation; those who speak in another language are to be two or at the most three, in turn, with one interpreting, and if there is no interpreter the speaker is to keep silent in the church and speak to himself and to God.

# JOB 3. Discernment

**The statement to replace:** "I decide something is off about a person before I could say what it
is." (+1)

**Settled against the passage.** 1 Corinthians 12:10 says “discerning of spirits”. 14:29 says “Let
two or three of the prophets speak, and let the others discern.” 1 John 4:1 says “test the spirits,
whether they are of God”, and gives the reason in the same verse: many false prophets have gone out
into the world. 1 Thessalonians 5:20-21 puts “Don’t despise prophecies” beside “Test all things,
and hold firmly that which is good.” In all four the object is what is behind something said: a
prophecy, a teaching, a claim to speak for God. None is a first impression of someone's character.
So the two reviewers are right that the statement must go, and the Pentecostal reviewer keeps what
he asked for in a different way: the replacement says nothing about HOW the reader knew. A reader
who holds that the Spirit showed them and a reader who holds that they checked it against the Bible
can both agree with the same sentence, because it reports what they did and what came of it.

| id | statement | dir | words | the risk I see |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | I have warned people off something said or taught in God’s name, and they later told me I was right. | +1 | 20 | What happened, then what others later said; silent on how. "In God’s name" is my plainest wording for a claim to speak for God, and it is not on the jargon list, but a newcomer critic should read it. A reader who warns against everything will have some hits to remember. Partly depends on other people, but the reader began it, so it caps the newcomer less than "people come to me" does. |
| D2 | I have said that something taught in God’s name was not to be trusted while others were welcoming it. | +1 | 19 | The act and its cost (being the only one), with no claim to have been right. A contrarian, or the prejudiced reader the audit worried about, agrees strongly, though now about teachings and not about people. |
| D3 | I am slower than the people around me to accept that a message or a claim is from God. | +1 | 19 | A true trade-off: the slow reader sometimes guards the church and sometimes does what 1 Thessalonians 5:19-20 warns against. But it ranks the reader against other people, which the first edit refused for faith, and a Pentecostal will say the gift also recognises what IS from God, quickly. |

**Pick: D1.**

**The other two statements, reviewed for the same fault:**
- "I take a new idea as it sounds rather than look into where it came from." (-1) **Keep.** Its
  object is an idea and its act is testing, which is the passage. No change.
- "Friends ask me whether someone can be trusted before they rely on them." (+1) **Same fault,
  milder.** Its object is a person's character, and it rewards being the friend whose read on
  people is sought, which is the hunch about people at one remove. Reword, keeping it as
  discernment's one "others" statement:
  **"Friends ask me whether a teaching or a claim holds up before they go along with it."** (+1,
  17 words.) Risk: nearer than before to wisdom's "people ask me which one to take" and to the
  spare knowledge statement; what separates them is that this asks whether something is to be
  trusted, not which option to take and not where the Bible says it.

**The summary.** "These statements can see only that everyday pattern" is no longer the right
sentence: the new statements are about the thing the passage names. Proposed:
> The passage names the discerning of spirits. Paul tells the church to let “the others discern”
> when prophets speak (1 Corinthians 14:29), and John writes, “test the spirits, whether they are
> of God” (1 John 4:1). These statements ask whether you have weighed something said or taught in
> God’s name, and what came of it; they cannot see how you knew, and they are not about first
> impressions of people. Christians who hold that some of the gifts in this sentence of Paul’s were
> for the time of the apostles do not all agree on whether this is one of them.

The four recorded acts can stay. Two of them (Simon, Elymas) are addressed to a person, but in both
the matter is what the person was doing with the message: buying it, and turning the proconsul from
it. Acts 5:1-4: see the note under the word of knowledge.

**Wisdom and faith: the one sentence.** Both summaries should end with the same sentence as
discernment's:
> Christians who hold that some of the gifts in this sentence of Paul’s were for the time of the
> apostles do not all agree on whether this is one of them.

It is uncited. I believe it is true (the cessationist reviewer names writers who count these three
among the extraordinary gifts, and other cessationist writers treat them as continuing), but I
opened no book, so either the editor keeps it this general or somebody opens the books. Otherwise
I would leave wisdom's and faith's summaries alone: their statements still are the everyday
pattern, and their summaries already say so.
