# Gifts quiz — adversarial critique, plain language

**Who is reading:** someone who started coming to a church six months ago. I know the words
*church*, *pray*, *Bible*, *serve*. I do not know what a rota is, I have never been on one, and if a
statement makes me feel stupid I close the tab. I am reading these one at a time on a phone, with no
headings, no gift names, and no idea what any of them is "for".

**On the owner's instruction of 2026-09-21 ("Don't leave Jesus off the list. I want to include our
Savior"):** that governs the *figure* quiz's roster, and QUIZ-BLUEPRINTS.md already carries it with
the rule for how he is included. A gifts result names a gift, never a person, so there is no list
here to leave him off. Nothing in this critique touches that instruction or narrows it in any way —
it stays exactly as the blueprint records it.

---

## The verdict in one paragraph

Both drafts are better than I expected and neither is ready. Nobody broke the word limit (longest
statement in either draft is 19 words — machine-counted, all 130), nobody counted my prayers or my
giving to church, and the banned insider words are almost entirely absent: one hit, "season", in
A's `G-SHP-1`. ("Walk them through it" is ordinary English, not the insider *walk*.) What actually
goes wrong is smaller and more damaging: **pronouns with nothing to point at**, **statements a
sixteen-year-old or a housebound seventy-year-old physically cannot answer**, **two clauses glued
together with a comma or a colon**, and **the same question asked twice under two different gift
names**. Draft B writes better sentences; Draft A picks better behaviours. The final set has to be
mixed, not chosen.

---

## The six things that go wrong (read this before the item list)

**1. Pronouns with no antecedent. This is the worst bug in either draft.** The reader sees ONE
statement at a time. Anything that points back to a neighbouring row is broken on a phone.

- B `evangelism-5`: "Starting **that conversation** with a stranger…" — *what* conversation? It only
  makes sense if you just read `evangelism-1`. As shipped, this is unanswerable.
- B `evangelism-3`: "People who are not religious tell me I am easy to ask **about it**." — about
  what? My job? My tattoo?
- B `faith-2`: "People say I am still expecting **it** to work out…" — *it* never appears.
- A `G-EVG-5`: "…than hear me talk **about it**." — same fault, milder.

**2. The teenager and the housebound reader cannot answer large parts of this.**

- Money: A `G-GIV-2` ("a chunk of **what I earn**") and `G-GIV-4` ("my own **finances**") assume a
  wage. A fifteen-year-old, a student, a pensioner and someone on benefits all stall. B's giving
  items, which say "money" and "things", are the right model.
- Physical work: **every single serving item in both drafts is lifting, carrying or tidying**
  (A `G-SRV-1/2/3`, B `serving-1/2/4/5`). A person who cannot lift a chair scores zero on serving no
  matter how much of their week goes on other people. That is a hole in the instrument, not a
  finding about them. The editor needs at least one serving item that is not manual labour.
- Housing: the whole hospitality category assumes you control a front door. A teenager in their
  parents' house, a lodger, someone in a care home — none of them can host. B `hospitality-3` ("I
  add a seat for whoever turns up") is the one item in either draft that survives this, because
  making room is not the same as owning the room.
- Work: A `G-LED-2` "even when it was not **my meeting**" is an office. I have never chaired a
  meeting in my life.

**3. Two claims stapled together.** If I agree with the first half and not the second, I cannot
answer. Offenders: A `G-ADM-4`, `G-WIS-4`, `G-GIV-5`, `G-MCY-2` (three claims); B `leading-3`,
`discernment-1` (the colon is a join), `giving-5`.

**4. Statements where disagreeing makes me sound like a bad person.** Rule 3 says both ends must
cost something. These fail it: A `G-MCY-3` (disagreeing = "I am one of the people who stopped
calling"), A `G-MCY-1` and B `mercy-1` (disagreeing = "I leave people alone in the worst of it"),
B `discernment-1` ("hard to sell to" is a compliment fishing for a yes), A `G-ENC-2`.

**5. The same question twice under two different gift names.** This is worse than boring — it makes
two gifts rise together off one answer, and the reader notices they were asked the same thing.
Listed in full in its own section below.

**6. Abstractions where a behaviour was promised.** "I **assume** people know where they stand"
(A `G-ENC-4`), "I **assume** most people would rather work it out" (A `G-TCH-5`), "test what is
going on **underneath**" (A `G-DSC-5`). Rule 1 asks what I *do* or what people *said*. What I assume
about other people's preferences is neither.

## Thirty-nine statements is too many on a phone

Thirteen gifts × three is 39, which Draft B calls "about four minutes". Four minutes of thumb-work
before you see anything is a lot for a quiz nobody has played yet, and by statement 25 I am reading
