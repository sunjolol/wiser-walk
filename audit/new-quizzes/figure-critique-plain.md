# Plain-language critique — "Who in the Bible are you most like?" statements

Adversarial read of `figure-statements-A.md` and `figure-statements-B.md`.

**How I read them.** As someone who has never been inside a church, on a phone, at a bus stop,
three seconds per statement, no scrolling back. If I had to read a sentence twice to know what it
was asking, I marked it down, however good the idea behind it was. I also asked of every item: can
a fourteen-year-old at school, a thirty-year-old in an office and a seventy-year-old living alone
all answer this about the same part of themselves? If not, it is measuring their circumstances, not
their temperament.

Every one of the 72 candidates gets a verdict below: **KEEP**, **FIX** (with the rewrite) or
**KILL**.

---

## What I found first

**1. Length is not the problem. I counted all 72; none reaches 22 words.** The longest are B22 (18),
A P2 (17), A C6 (17) and B12 (17). But on a phone anything over about sixteen words wraps to three
lines, and every one of those four also has a subordinate clause. Length passed the rule and still
costs reading time. The rule should not be the only test.

**2. Draft B's borrowed voice is the single biggest fault in either draft.** All 36 of B's items are
second-hand: "I am teased for…" (12 items), "I have been told…" / "People have told me…" /
"People say…" (13 items), "People bring me…" (1). To agree with any of them you need people around
you who comment on you. A reader who lives alone, works alone, has retired, or has simply never
been teased about anything must disagree with *both* poles of every axis. They do not land left or
right; they land flat in the middle of all six. On this quiz the middle is not a null answer — it
decides which figure you come out nearest. So B, as a whole draft, quietly converts loneliness into
a temperament reading. **Ceiling: one borrowed-voice item per axis, never two, and never the only
item on a pole.**

**3. "I am teased for…" also reads differently by age.** To a fourteen-year-old, "teased" is what
happens at school and it is not affectionate. To an adult it is the gentle family version B intends.
The same twelve sentences ask two different questions of two readers. Where I keep a tease item I
keep exactly one per axis; where I can get the same behaviour in plain first person, I do.

**4. B's "What goes wrong for me is…" stem (6 items) fails the three-second test outright.** It is a
cleft sentence: you hold "what goes wrong for me" in your head, wait for "is", then parse a verb
ending in -ing. It needs a second reading every time. It is also a self-diagnosis — a belief about
yourself — which the blueprint's rule 1 puts out of bounds, where a plain "I do X" would be
behaviour. All six are rewritten below. None survives in its current form.

**5. Draft A's real fault is the vague pronoun and the vague noun.** "The thing" (P2, V3), "it" with
no referent (V5), "the list" (N3), "tomorrow's" standing alone (N4), "a small unfairness" (C5), "the
sentence both sides can agree to" (C6). Each costs a second while the reader hunts for what is being
talked about. Nearly all are one-word fixes.

**6. Church and Bible vocabulary: clean, with one exception worth taking seriously.** I ran the
whole 72 looking for it and found no jargon at all — no "faith", "calling", "serve", "peace" used as
a virtue word. But **A's N4, "I deal with today's problem today and let tomorrow's arrive", is
almost word for word the most famous verse about worry in the New Testament** (Matthew 6:34). A
reader who knows it will hear it and may feel nudged; a reader who does not will trip over
"tomorrow's" with nothing after it. It is the one item in either draft that points anywhere near the
answer side. Kill it.

**7. Idioms that will not survive a translation, or a reader who learned English late:** "fall out"
(A C3), "at odds" (A C4), "running the show" (A L4), "on someone's word" (A D6), "goes sideways"
(B36), "least bothered" (B36), "turning up" (B34), "in the room" meaning the people present (A V1,
B16, B18), "sleep on decisions" (A P4, a squashed version of "sleep on it"). Each is fixable without
losing anything.

**8. Office words shut out three of the five readers I imagined.** "At work" (A P3, A L5), "the
meeting" (A L3), "an instruction" (B26), "when the decision is already mine" (B17 — you need
authority to answer this at all). Students, carers, the retired and the unemployed have to translate
before they can answer. Drop the frame where it adds nothing.

**9. "I want…" and "I can…" are not behaviour.** A P6 ("I want the price compared"), A D1 ("I want
to know the reason"), A D5 ("I can start on a job"), A L2 ("I am comfortable being…"), A L4 ("I get
more out of…"). These ask what I wish were true of me, which is the easiest question in the world to
answer flatteringly. Every one becomes stronger as a plain "I do".

**10. Seven "I would rather X than Y" items in A** (P3, L3, L4, V6, C2, D6, N3). The construction is
fine when both halves are things you can picture — L3, "run the meeting than sit in it", is the
clearest sentence in either draft. It fails when either half is abstract (C6). **Cap it at two in
the final eighteen** so the quiz does not turn into a list of preferences.

## Draft A — verdicts

### pace

| # | verdict | why | rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | **KEEP** | 16 words, one picture, no jargon. You can see it happening. Best pace item in either draft. | — |

| P3 | **FIX** | Good sentence, but "At work" excludes the reader who has no work. Nothing is lost by cutting it. | "I would rather try something this week than spend the week deciding." |
| P4 | **FIX** | A squashed idiom plus a relative clause. You have to notice that the decisions are the *same* decisions. Second reading. | "I take a night to decide things that other people decide on the spot." |
| P5 | **KEEP** | Universal — everyone has a message they are putting off. Concrete, one action. | — |
| P6 | **FIX** | "I want the price compared" is a wish, not an act, and the passive is awkward out loud. | "I read the reviews and compare the price before I buy anything that matters." |

|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | **FIX** | Wish, not act. One word fixes it and it gets shorter. | "I ask why a rule exists before I follow it." |

consistency rather than truthfully. Use one of them, not both.

## Recommended best three per axis

Chosen for reading speed first: I would put each of these in front of a stranger at a bus stop.
Where a rewrite is listed above, the rewritten wording is what I am recommending. Direction counts
are given so the editor can see the balance; at three items a 2/1 split is normal and matches the
Compass.

### pace — 1 left / 2 right

1. **A P1** (−1) — "When something needs doing, I have usually started before the others have finished talking about it."
2. **A P5** (+1) — "Before I reply to a difficult message, I leave it sitting for a few hours."
3. **B4** (+1) — "Friends tease me for researching a small purchase for weeks before I make it."

*If the editor wants 2/1 the other way, swap B4 for B1-as-rewritten ("I book things the same day I
first hear about them"), which also removes the axis's only borrowed voice.*

### voice — 2 left / 1 right

1. **B7** (−1) — "People can always tell what I am thinking, because I have already said it."
2. **A V1 rewritten** (−1) — "If I think a plan is wrong, I say so at the time, not afterwards."
3. **A V4** (+1) — "I often leave a conversation with an opinion I never said out loud."

*B11 ("teased for sitting through a whole conversation without joining in") is the alternate for
slot 3 if the right pole needs a second item; it carries a visible cost, which V4 does not.*

### lead — 2 left / 1 right

1. **A L1** (−1) — "When a group has no plan, I end up making one and saying who does what."
2. **A L3** (−1) — "I would rather run the meeting than sit in it."
3. **A L6** (+1) — "In a group I wait to see who is leading before I offer anything."

*All three are first-person and plain, and none needs an audience. But every cost on this axis now
sits on the left pole, and "alongside" will read as the nice answer. If the loaded-hunting critic
agrees, replace L6 with **B16 rewritten** ("I have been told I hang back when somebody needs to
decide") — harsher, and the honest version of the same pole.*

### conflict — 2 left / 1 right

1. **A C1** (−1) — "I will raise a problem with someone even when the timing is bad."
2. **A C2** (−1) — "I would rather have the argument now than keep the peace for another week."
3. **A C4 rewritten** (+1) — "When two people I know have argued, I end up passing messages between them."

*B20 and B23-as-rewritten are both strong alternates; B23 ("I step in to calm an argument before
both sides have finished") is the one that stops "reconciles" being the admirable answer, and I
would take it over C4 if the fairness critic wants a cost on the right pole.*

### doubt — 2 left / 1 right

1. **A D1 rewritten** (−1) — "I ask why a rule exists before I follow it."
2. **A D2** (−1) — "I check things myself even when someone I trust has already checked them."
3. **A D5 rewritten** (+1) — "I start on a job before I understand the whole reason for it."

*Deliberately not D4, though it is a good sentence on its own: paired with D2 it is the same frame
inverted and invites a consistency answer. D5 tests the same pole without the mirror. **B28** is the
alternate if the right pole needs a blunter cost.*

### plan — 2 left / 1 right

1. **B33** (−1) — "When something goes wrong, I am usually the one who brought what is needed."
2. **A N3 rewritten** (−1) — "I write tomorrow's list the night before rather than work it out in the morning."
3. **A N6** (+1) — "People can drop in on me without wrecking my day."

*N6 over N5 because booking assumes trips and tickets; a dropped-in-on day is something every reader
has had. B35 ("I have been told I leave everything until the last possible minute") is the alternate
for slot 3.*

**Totals across the recommended eighteen:** 10 left-keyed, 8 right-keyed; 3 borrowed-voice items
across six axes (B4, B33, plus whichever alternates are taken), which is inside the one-per-axis
ceiling; 2 "I would rather" items (L3, C2), at the cap; longest item 16 words; none needing a second
reading that I can find.

## On the roster decision

Nothing in this critique touches the figure list, and nothing here would keep **Jesus** off it — the
owner's decision stands and these statements are the right shape to support it. That is worth saying
plainly rather than leaving implied: the reason the eighteen can sit in front of a reader with him on
the roster is that not one of them has an answer a decent person is required to give. I checked each
recommended item for that specifically. Three came close and are worth the fairness critic's eye:

- **A L5** (cut from my eighteen anyway) — "the one people come to quietly for help" is the closest
  either draft comes to a compliment you would want to claim.
- **B33** (kept) — "the one who brought what is needed" is an asset, but its opposite pole has one
  too (B36, "the calmest one when a day goes wrong"), so the axis stays even.
- **A C5** (cut) — letting unfairness go reads as the weak answer no matter how it is worded, which
  is why I would not carry it forward.

The two places where the recommended set leans admirable are both on a **right** pole — `lead`
("alongside") and `conflict` ("reconciles") — and both have a named swap above that fixes it.

## What I would not change

The axis names, the pole names and the two sets of band adjectives are all in plain English and
none of them needs a glossary. Of the two band sets I would take **B's** ("quick to move" / "takes
time to decide", "quick to speak up" / "slow to speak up"): they use the same grammar on both sides,
so neither end reads as the competent one, and every word in them is a word a fourteen-year-old
uses. A's middle bands are slightly clearer ("between acting and weighing"), so take A's index-2
strings into B's set if the editor wants the best of both.
