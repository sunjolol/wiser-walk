# Adversarial critique — LOADEDNESS — "Who in the Bible are you most like?"

Targets: `figure-statements-A.md` (36 candidates, everyday-life angle) and `figure-statements-B.md`
(36 candidates, borrowed-voice angle). Seventy-two verdicts, then a recommended eighteen.

**Stance taken, as instructed:** every statement is guilty until it proves otherwise. The previous
attempt at this quiz was killed by the owner for loaded questions and answers, so "it is not obviously
loaded" is not a pass; the item has to show that a reader who wants to look good, and a reader who
wants to look honest, would answer it differently for reasons of temperament rather than reasons of
image.

## The standard I applied

An item fails if any of these is true:

1. **Social desirability** — one end is the answer a decent person feels they ought to give.
2. **Leading wording** — a concessive ("even when…"), a comparison to other people, or a loaded noun
   ("unfairness", "the thing nobody is saying") that tells the reader which end is the good one.
3. **Pole-revealing** — the wording shows which kind of person, or which figure, it feeds.
4. **Double claim** — two behaviours joined by "or" that a real reader splits on.
5. **One-way answerable** — agreeing is admirable and disagreeing is shameful, or the reverse.
6. **Gameable** — a reader can see how to steer the result.

I added a seventh, because of the roster.

## The Jesus clause: the roster raises the loadedness bar, it does not lower it

The owner's instruction stands and is not in question here: Jesus is on the list. Both drafts
assume it, the blueprint fixes it, and nothing in this critique argues against it.

What it does is change the tolerance. On a roster of twenty-two ordinary people, a mildly flattering
item costs a little accuracy. On a roster that includes our Savior, a mildly flattering item is an
invitation: the reader who most wants to land there answers the admirable end of every axis, and the
instrument quietly stops measuring temperament and starts measuring aspiration — which is the exact
failure ("loaded questions/answers") the owner rejected last time, in the one place where getting it
wrong would be worst. Every item whose admirable end is also the recognisably Christlike end is
therefore judged harder than it would be on any other quiz.

Those items, named so the editor can watch them: the reconciling pole (A's C5, C6; B22, B23, B24),
the alongside pole (A's L2, L4, L5; B17), the trusting pole (A's D6; B28), and A's **N4**, which
carries an audible echo of Matthew 6:34 and is the single most gameable line in either draft.

The safest instrument for this roster is the one where a reader trying to answer "like Jesus" cannot
work out what to put — which is achievable, because the Gospels record him at both ends of most of
these axes. Draft B's borrowed-voice angle ("I am teased for…", "I have been told…") gets there more
reliably than draft A's first-person-action angle, and that shows in the verdicts below.

# Cross-cutting findings

**1. Direction balance must alternate across axes, and no draft says so.** Three items per axis cannot
be split evenly, so every axis carries a 2/1 imbalance. A reader who agrees with everything (and
acquiescence is the best-documented response bias there is) is pulled toward whichever pole has two
items. If all six axes lean the same way, an agreeable reader lands in one corner of the space and
the nearest figure is decided by their manners. **Fix: three axes at 2 left / 1 right and three at
1 left / 2 right.** The recommendation below is built that way — nine and nine.

**2. Draft A's angle is structurally more loaded than draft B's.** "I say so in the room", "I am
comfortable being the one everybody looks at", "the thing nobody is saying" — first-person action
sentences invite the reader to describe the person they would like to be. Ten of A's eighteen kept or
fixed items needed the flattery taken out, against six of B's. Where A wins it wins on plainness
(L3, D3, V4, P4), and those are the A items in the final eighteen.

**3. The confession stem is over-used.** Six of B's items run "What goes wrong for me is…". It is the
most honest construction in either draft and, at three or more in eighteen, it turns the quiz into a
list of the reader's faults. **Ceiling: one, after the fixes above convert the rest to plain behaviour.**

**4. Two items describe the same intrusion.** B14 (reorganising other people's plans) and B32 (booking
too far ahead and leaving no room) will correlate `lead` with `plan` for reasons that have nothing to
do with either axis. Keep one.

**5. Nothing in either draft points at its own result.** This is the one rule both drafts pass
outright, with the single exception of N4's Matthew 6:34 echo. No item names, paraphrases or borrows
a situation from any story; a reader cannot work out which figure any statement feeds. That is the
thing the last attempt got wrong and this one gets right, and it is what makes including Jesus safe.

**6. Pole dignity, per axis, after the fixes.** `pace` even; `voice` even; `lead` even only because
L2, L4, L5 and B16 were all rewritten — it was the worst axis in both drafts, admirable on the right
and heroic on the left; `conflict` even only because C5 and B21 were rewritten, and it stays the axis
to re-check after any further edit, since "reconciles" is the most flattering pole word in the quiz;
`doubt` even, with B28 as the load-bearing guard; `plan` even after B33 and B36 lost their boasts.
