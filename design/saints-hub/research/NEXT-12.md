# The next twelve saint pages (research of 2026-09-25)

## HIS RULING (2026-09-26): saints before about 1400, ideally before 700; no modern saints

In his words: "I am not interested in modern saints. I mostly want to stick to saints before the year 1400 or so,
ideally before 700, but I'd of course make exceptions up to the year 1400 for truly remarkable saints. We can maybe
revisit modern saints later but for now it's too big of a headache and not the type of content I want at this moment."

So section (c) below is settled (option 1: modern saints stay out), and two of the twelve leave the list:
**Teresa of Ávila** (died 1582) and **Thérèse of Lisieux** (died 1897). Proposed in their place, both women, from the
reserves: **Perpetua and Felicity** (died 203: her own prison diary, ideal by his rule) and **Catherine of Siena**
(died 1380, a Doctor of the Church: a "truly remarkable" exception before 1400). Alternates: Helena (died about 330,
the True Cross) and Olga of Kyiv (died 969). **Joan of Arc died in 1431**, just past his line: ask him.
The other ten stand: Nicholas, Ignatius of Antioch, Francis of Assisi (an exception: died 1226), Athanasius, Thomas
Aquinas (an exception: died 1274), Patrick, Mary of Egypt, George, Moses the Black, and Joan if he keeps her.

The hub has 23 full pages. Five more are planned for the personality test (Monica, Cuthbert, Guthlac, Philip Neri,
Arsenius). The owner wants 12 more, to reach 40. This file picks them.

How they were picked:
- **Demand:** English Wikipedia views, Sep 2025 to Aug 2026, humans only. Views are a rough guide, not search volume.
- **The "why not X?" test:** would a visitor wonder why the name is missing?
- **The gap:** are the pages that rank today thin, devotional or unsourced?
- **The rules:** a public-domain English text to quote word for word, and a public-domain or CC0 picture with a face.
  Every picture below was checked against its Commons licence tag on 2026-09-25.
- **Balance:** East and West, men and women, every era.

For scale, the five planned people: Monica 161,809; Cuthbert 144,211; Philip Neri 89,532; Arsenius 13,009. Guthlac's
number could not be read (the title fetched is probably a redirect).

What the site can win on every page below: **both calendars on one page**, **a source for every line**, **"Often
misquoted"**, and **a sober "Is he a saint?"**. The pages ranking today rarely do any of the four.

### 1. Nicholas of Myra (1,027,651 views)
- **Why:** the most searched pre-modern saint after Joan. Honoured East and West. "Why no St Nicholas?" is the
  first question a visitor would ask.
- **Hook:** how a 4th-century bishop became Santa Claus. Second hook: did he really slap Arius at Nicaea? (No. The
  earliest account is from about 1370 and says "an Arian", and Arius was not a bishop.)
- **The record:** rich in stories, thin in facts. He wrote nothing. The oldest story (the three generals) is 5th or
  6th century; the first full Life is 9th century. The page should say so plainly and tell the stories as stories.
- **Sources (public domain):**
  - The Golden Legend (Caxton, ed. Ellis 1900): https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/goldenlegend/gl-vol2-nicholas.asp
  - Catholic Encyclopedia (1911): https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11063b.htm
  - The road to Santa, all public domain: Washington Irving's *Knickerbocker's History of New York* (1809) and
    Clement C. Moore's "A Visit from St. Nicholas" (1823).
  - The Arius story's evidence: https://www.roger-pearse.com/weblog/2015/02/28/did-st-nicholas-of-myra-santa-claus-punch-arius-at-the-council-of-nicaea/
  - The dating of each legend: https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/stories-legends/classic-sources
    (use for dates only; its modern translations are not public domain).
- **Picture:** icon by Aleksa Petrov, 1294, 1400x1941, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nikola_from_1294.jpg . Alternative: Sinai icon, 13th century,
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:St_Nicholas_Icon_Sinai_13th_century.jpg .
- **Competition:** stnicholascenter.org is the best single-saint site on the web. Do not try to out-write it; win on
  the calendars (6 December; 19 December Old Calendar; 9 May, the relics to Bari), the legend-versus-evidence layout
  and the myths. The rest (OCA, New Advent, Core Christianity, FOCUS) are short or single-tradition.
- **Risk:** thin facts. The page must not dress legend as history.

### 2. Ignatius of Antioch (292,958 views)
- **Why:** an Apostolic Father. Polycarp is live, and the "Apostolic Fathers" group needs him. Research is already
  gathered (`design/quiz-ideas/fathers/research/figures/group-01.json`, researcher-checked only: 7 stories, 9 lines,
  9 misattributions, 5 pictures). A checking pass is needed before publishing.
- **Hook:** a bishop in chains writing seven letters on the road to the lions, begging Rome not to save him ("I am
  the wheat of God"). The first surviving use of "the catholic Church".
- **Sources (public domain):** the seven letters, *Ante-Nicene Fathers* vol. 1 (Roberts and Donaldson), on New Advent
  (e.g. https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0107.htm for Romans); Eusebius, *Church History* book 3; Chrysostom's
  homily on Ignatius (*Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series vol. 9); the later *Martyrdom of Ignatius*
  (ANF 1), marked as later.
- **Picture:** Menologion of Basil II, about 985, 1949x1341, Public domain (a scene with the lions; the face is small).
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ignatius_of_Antioch.jpg . The research file lists four more.
- **Competition:** Britannica, CPH blog, Christianity.com, Notre Dame, Learn Religions. All short.
- **Risks:** which letters are genuine (the long, middle and short versions): say it plainly. His stress on the
  bishop is read differently by Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants: give each reading.
- **Calendars:** 17 October (Catholic, Anglican); 20 December and 29 January (Orthodox).

### 3. Francis of Assisi (979,929 views)
- **Why:** the most famous saint of the West. Anglicans and Lutherans keep him too (4 October).
- **Hook:** the two most famous "Francis" lines are not his. The "Prayer of St Francis" ("Make me an instrument of
  your peace") first appeared in a French magazine in 1912. "Preach the gospel at all times; use words if necessary"
  is in none of his writings. Then the real story: the leper, the naked renunciation before the bishop, the sultan
  in 1219, the stigmata, the Canticle of the Sun.
- **Sources (public domain):**
  - His own writings (Paschal Robinson, 1906): https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Writings_of_St._Francis_of_Assisi
  - Thomas of Celano's Lives (Howell, 1908): https://archive.org/details/livesofsfranciso00thom
  - The Legend of the Three Companions: https://archive.org/details/legendofsaintfra00unse
  - The Little Flowers (the wolf of Gubbio, the sermon to the birds): https://sacred-texts.com/chr/lff/index.htm
  - The 1912 prayer: https://franciscan-archive.org/franciscana/peace.html
  - Kindred research already checked some lines (`research/kindred-research-hearth-spark.json`).
- **Picture:** Cimabue, 1278 to 1280, the oldest likeness in paint, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cimabue_-_Madonna_Enthroned_with_the_Child,_St_Francis_and_four_Angels_(detail)_-_WGA04921.jpg
  (762x930). Larger scans of the same fresco: `File:Cimabue_019.jpg` (2024x2464); confirm which crop shows him.
- **Competition:** Franciscan Media, Biography.com, Catholic Online, Notre Dame, National Shrine. Devotional; none
  give sources or the misattributions in one place.
- **Risk:** the Orthodox view. He is not an Orthodox saint and some Orthodox writers criticise his mysticism. Say so
  in "Is he a saint?", in both sides' words.

### 4. Joan of Arc (2,264,873 views)
- **Why:** the highest demand of any saint before the 20th century. Canonised 1920. The Church of England and the
  Episcopal Church also commemorate her on 30 May (check both calendars).
- **Hook:** her trial. An unlettered girl of 19 against dozens of theologians. Asked if she knew she was in God's
  grace (a trap either way), she answered: if not, may God put her there; if so, may God keep her there. Burned in
  1431, cleared in 1456.
- **Sources (public domain):** T. Douglas Murray, *Jeanne d'Arc, Maid of Orleans* (1902): the condemnation trial, the
  rehabilitation witnesses and her letters, in English. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/57389 and
  https://archive.org/details/jeannedarcmaidof00murruoft . One of the richest records of any medieval life. (The
  famous-trials.com transcript uses another translation: quote Murray.)
- **Picture:** Ingres, *Joan of Arc at the Coronation of Charles VII*, 1854, 3840x5176, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ingres_coronation_charles_vii.jpg . CC0 alternative: Ingres, about 1844,
  National Gallery of Art, 4000x3095:
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jean-Auguste-Dominique_Ingres,_Joan_of_Arc_Standing_at_the_Altar_at_Reims_Cathedral,_c._1844,_NGA_125528.jpg .
  No portrait from life exists (a clerk's doodle in the margin of a 1429 register is the only drawing made in her
  lifetime, and the clerk never saw her): a good tidbit.
- **Competition:** History.com, Britannica, Wikipedia, CNA's "15 quotes from her trial". Good but scattered.
- **Risks:** the voices (skeptics' medical theories), the men's clothing charge, and her use by politicians since.
  State them plainly; do not take sides beyond the record.

### 5. Athanasius of Alexandria (234,035 views)
- **Why:** completes the "great teachers" group (the four great Eastern Doctors). Wrote the *Life of Antony* (links to
  Antony's page). His Easter letter of 367 is the first list of exactly our 27 New Testament books. Research is
  gathered (`group-04.json`, researcher-checked only: 8 stories, 7 lines, 4 misattributions, 5 pictures).
- **Hook:** five exiles in 45 years. Accused of murdering a bishop, he produced the "dead" man alive at the council.
  Fleeing Julian's agents by boat, he turned back to meet them; asked where Athanasius was, he said "not far off".
  Note: "Athanasius contra mundum" is a later phrase, and the Athanasian Creed is not his (both already in research).
- **Sources (public domain):** *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series vol. 4 (On the Incarnation, Life of
  Antony, Apologies, Festal Letter 39) at New Advent, e.g. https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2802.htm ; Gregory
  Nazianzen's Oration 21 on him: https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310221.htm ; Theodoret, *Church History* book 3
  (the boat): https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/27023.htm ; Socrates and Sozomen (NPNF 2/2).
- **Picture:** icon from Sozopol, 17th century, 1704x2372, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saint-Athanasius-of-Alexandria-icon-Sozopol-Bulgaria-17century.jpg
- **Competition:** mostly sermons and opinion pieces on "contra mundum" (Desiring God, Catholic Answers, Substack).
  Few plain life pages.
- **Calendars:** 2 May (Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran); 18 January and 2 May (Orthodox).
- **Risk:** the violence charged against his party in Alexandria. State the charges and his answers.

### 6. Thomas Aquinas (967,292 views)
- **Why:** the greatest name of medieval theology. Anglicans keep 28 January too.
- **Hook:** "the dumb ox"; his family locked him in a castle to stop him becoming a friar; and at the end: "all that
  I have written seems to me like straw", after which he never wrote again.
- **Sources (public domain):** the *Summa* (English Dominican Province, 1920): https://www.newadvent.org/summa/ ; the
  *Summa contra Gentiles* (Rickaby, 1905); his hymns (Adoro te, Pange lingua) in 19th-century translations; the
  Catholic Encyclopedia's life (1912), which tells the ox, the firebrand and the straw:
  https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14663b.htm . The straw line comes from a witness at his canonisation inquiry
  (1319): quote it only from a public-domain English life, and say it is reported.
- **Picture:** Carlo Crivelli, 1476, 4004x6000, Public domain. https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:St-thomas-aquinas.jpg
- **Competition:** Britannica, the philosophy encyclopedias (heavy), Christian History, Christianity.com. The stories
  are told in pieces; the hard topics rarely.
- **Risks, to state plainly:** he did not hold the Immaculate Conception; he wrote that heretics may be put to death;
  his view of women follows Aristotle. The Orthodox do not count him a saint.

### 7. Patrick (942,608 views)
- **Why:** huge demand every March. Honoured East and West (Orthodox churches keep 17 March too). A rare saint whose
  own account of his life survives.
- **Hook:** "Who was St Patrick really?" He was British, not Irish. Kidnapped at 16, a slave for six years, he ran
  away after a dream and went back as a bishop. The snakes (Ireland had none; Roman writers already said so), the
  shamrock (first linked to him in writing in the 18th century) and the Breastplate prayer (probably later) are
  "Often misquoted" gold.
- **Sources (public domain):** his *Confession* and *Letter to Coroticus*, tr. Newport J. D. White (1920), with
  Muirchú's 7th-century Life: https://ccel.org/ccel/patrick/confession and https://archive.org/details/StPatrick1920
- **Picture:** Giovanni Battista Tiepolo, *St Patrick, Bishop of Ireland*, 1746, 1315x2498, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tiepolo-s.patrizio.jpg
- **Competition:** History.com, Biography.com, The Conversation, the National Shrine, Life.Church. Decent, but they
  rarely quote the Confession by chapter.
- **Risk:** his dates (the "two Patricks" theory). Give the range and say why it is uncertain.

### 8. Mary of Egypt (87,971 views)
- **Why:** the best-loved woman saint of the Orthodox year after the Mother of God: the fifth Sunday of Lent carries
  her name, and her Life is read in church that week. Commonly searched and poorly served. It is the owner's
  "searched but few good pages" case.
- **Hook:** a teenager who ran away to Alexandria, lived 17 years in sin, was stopped at the door of the church in
  Jerusalem by an unseen force, and lived 47 years alone in the desert. The monk Zosimas found her, and a lion helped
  bury her.
- **Sources (public domain):**
  - The Old English Life with W. W. Skeat's translation (1890), which follows the Latin of Sophronius's Life closely:
    https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/%C3%86lfric%27s_Lives_of_Saints/Death_of_St._Mary_of_Egypt
  - The Golden Legend's "Mary Egyptian" (Fordham, as above); Catholic Encyclopedia:
    https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09763a.htm ; a map of every version: https://florianjanner.de/MaryOfEgypt/sources.html
  - No public-domain English translation of the Greek Life was found; the modern ones are in copyright.
- **Picture:** Jusepe de Ribera, 1651, 5406x6806, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:S._Maria_Egiziaca_by_Jusepe_de_Ribera.jpg . An icon to pair with it:
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Icon_of_Mary_of_Egypt_(Mstera,_19th_c.).jpg (Public domain, 1053x1258).
  CC0: Franceschini's *Last Communion of Saint Mary of Egypt* (the Met).
- **Competition:** Notre Dame, OrthodoxWiki, U.S. Catholic, GOARCH, parish pages. Thin or devotional.
- **Calendars:** 1 April and the fifth Sunday of Lent (Orthodox; 18 April 2027); 1 April in the 2004 Roman
  Martyrology (older Catholic books give 2 or 3 April).
- **Risks:** her dates and how much is history (the Life says the story came down by word of mouth). Her past must be
  told soberly.

### 9. George (1,008,378 views)
- **Why:** one of the two or three most honoured martyrs anywhere: the "Great Martyr" of the Orthodox, patron of
  England, Georgia and many more. Leaving him out fails the "why not X?" test at once.
- **Hook:** "Was St George real, and where did the dragon come from?" A real soldier-martyr, probably under
  Diocletian. A list credited to Pope Gelasius (said to be 494, probably later) kept his Acts out of church reading.
  The dragon joined the story in the 11th century, some 700 years after his death. The early Coptic Acts are wilder
  than the dragon: killed three times, he rises each time.
- **Sources (public domain):** E. A. Wallis Budge, *The Martyrdom and Miracles of Saint George of Cappadocia* (1888),
  the Coptic Acts in English: https://archive.org/details/martyrdommiracle00budguoft ; the Golden Legend's George and
  the dragon: https://archive.org/details/thegoldenlegendv3stgeorge ; Eusebius, *Church History* book 8 (an unnamed
  man at Nicomedia who tore down the edict, later identified with George: say "later identified").
  Budge's *George of Lydda* (1930) is now public domain in the US too. Find a public-domain English translation of the
  Gelasian decree before quoting it.
- **Picture:** Novgorod school, 15th century, Tretyakov Gallery, 3888x5152, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saint_George_icon._Novgorod_icon-painting_school,_15th_cent..,_Veliky_Novgorod,_Russia._Tretyakov_Gallery.jpg
- **Competition:** Britannica, EWTN, National Catholic Register, Wikipedia. Good on "the dragon is legend"; weak on
  what the early Acts actually say.
- **Calendars:** 23 April (both; 6 May on the Old Calendar); 3 November (Orthodox).
- **Risk:** almost no facts. Like Nicholas, the page is "what we know, and what was told".

### 10. Teresa of Ávila (438,861 views)
- **Why:** the first woman named a Doctor of the Church (1970, with Catherine of Siena). Anglicans and Lutherans keep
  15 October too. Her autobiography is one of the great first-person records.
- **Hook:** "Christ has no body now but yours" is the most shared "Teresa" line, and it is not hers (it grew from an
  1888 sermon by the Methodist Mark Guy Pearse). Then the real woman: a lively girl who loved romances, 18 dry years of
  prayer, the Inquisition reading her book, and the convent reform she ran on the road.
- **Sources (public domain):** *The Life of St Teresa of Jesus* (David Lewis): https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8120/8120-h/8120-h.htm ;
  *Interior Castle* (Benedictines of Stanbrook, 1921): https://ccel.org/ccel/teresa/castle2 ; *The Way of Perfection*
  (Stanbrook, 1911); Longfellow's 19th-century translation of her bookmark poem ("Let nothing disturb thee").
- **Picture:** Juan de la Miseria, 1576, the only portrait from life, 800x1159, Public domain (below the 1200 px aim).
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Teresa_de_Jesús.jpg . Large alternative: Alfred Dehodencq, 1850s,
  4500x4735, Public domain: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Santa_Teresa_de_Jesús_(Ayuntamiento_de_Sevilla).jpg .
  Tidbit, if the source holds: she is said to have told the painter he had made her ugly. Check before using.
- **Competition:** EWTN, Catholic Online, Franciscan Media, Notre Dame. Devotional; Goodreads spreads the fake lines.
- **Risk:** her Jewish-convert grandfather and the Inquisition: a real, documented story; tell it straight.

### 11. Thérèse of Lisieux (528,505 views)
- **Why:** the most-loved modern Catholic saint whose words are free to quote. A Doctor (1997). Died at 24.
- **Hook:** the "Little Way"; and her last 18 months, which she describes as a night of faith, in her own words.
- **Sources (public domain):** *The Story of a Soul*, tr. Thomas N. Taylor (1912), with her letters, sayings and poems:
  https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16772 . Caution: Taylor translates the 1898 text her sister Pauline edited, not
  the manuscripts published in 1956. Label the edition on every line.
- **Picture:** photograph by her sister Céline, 15 April 1895, 565x720, tagged PD-US-expired.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Teresa-de-Lisieux.jpg . Larger: a childhood photograph, before 1881,
  2560x1557, Public domain: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Therese_lisieux_as_a_child.jpg .
  Licence note: Céline died in 1959, so in France her photographs stay protected until 2030. On Commons they are
  public domain in the US only. The owner should decide whether "public domain in the US" is enough.
- **Competition:** littleflower.org, Marian.org, parish pages. Warm and devotional; few sources.
- **Risk:** Catholic only (no Orthodox or Protestant calendar). Keep "Is she a saint?" short and honest.

### 12. Moses the Black (145,520 views)
- **Why:** the owner's second kind of pick: much discussed, badly served. A feature film came out on 30 January 2026
  (digital 13 March). Research is gathered (`group-05.json`, researcher-checked only: 8 stories, 6 lines, 2
  misattributions, 3 pictures). He fills the Desert Fathers group beside Antony, Arsenius and Cassian.
- **Hook:** a gang leader turned monk. The leaking basket of sand ("my sins run out behind me") when asked to judge a
  brother. He refused to fight the raiders who killed him.
- **Sources (public domain):** Palladius, *Lausiac History* ch. 19 (Lowther Clarke, 1918):
  https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/palladius_lausiac_02_text.htm ; Budge, *The Paradise of the Holy Fathers* (1907):
  https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Paradise/Volume_2/Book_1/Chapter_1 ; Sozomen, *Church History* 6.29 (NPNF 2/2);
  Cassian's first two Conferences are with an Abba Moses (whether he is the same man is debated: say so).
  Check which public-domain collection holds the basket story before quoting it (the research lists one
  misattributed "leaking jug" version).
- **Picture:** fresco, Dormition Cathedral of the Moscow Kremlin, 1479 to 1481, 2269x3247, Public domain.
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saint_Moses_the_Ethiopian_Fresco._Dormition_Cathedral_of_the_Moscow_Kremlin.jpg
  Most modern icons of him are in copyright: use only the old ones.
- **Competition:** Wikipedia, a short EWTN page, OrthodoxWiki, Newman Connection, blogs. A clear gap.
- **Calendars:** 28 August (Orthodox and the Roman Martyrology); the Coptic Church keeps him on 24 Paoni (1 July).
- **Risk:** the sayings record other monks mocking his colour to test him. State it plainly, in the sources' words.

## (b) Reserves, ranked

1. **Olga of Kyiv (416,574).** Honoured East and West; a woman; baptised in Constantinople. Hook: "the saint who took
   revenge" (her four revenges on the Drevlians are widely shared online, rarely explained). Sources: the *Primary
   Chronicle*: S. H. Cross's first translation (1930) is public domain in the US since 1 January 2026, but no online
   scan was found; the 1953 revision is listed as free to read at Google Books (check its copyright renewal before
   quoting). Pictures: Radziwiłł Chronicle miniatures, 15th century, Public domain but small (649x994); Vasnetsov's
   sketch, 447x1000. Risks: violence, Kyiv/Kiev naming and Russian and Ukrainian claims, small pictures. Could swap
   in for George or Moses.
2. **Catherine of Siena (381,987).** Doctor, woman, told a pope to go home to Rome. Hook: "Be who God meant you to be
   and you will set the world on fire" is a modern paraphrase; her letter says "you will set fire to all Italy".
   Sources: *The Dialogue* (Thorold, 1896), *Letters* (Scudder, 1905), Raymond of Capua's Life (check the translation's
   date). Pictures: plenty of public-domain panels (check a face). Risk: her extreme fasting, stated plainly.
3. **Thomas More (837,491).** Catholic and Anglican calendars. Hook: "the King's good servant, but God's first", and his
   jokes on the scaffold. Sources: William Roper's Life (https://archive.org/details/lifeofsirthomasm00ropeuoft),
   *Utopia*, his letters. Picture: Holbein, 1527, Public domain. Risks: he had heretics burned as Chancellor (state
   it); much of the traffic is Tudor history, where history sites are strong.
4. **Perpetua and Felicity (108,532).** Perpetua's prison diary is the earliest surviving writing by a Christian woman.
   Research ready (`group-02.json`). Source: ANF vol. 3, https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0324.htm . Risk: pictures
   are Ravenna and Poreč mosaics, about 700 px.
5. **Anthony of Padua (452,084).** Hook: patron of lost things; the sermon to the fish. Sources: the Little Flowers'
   chapters (public domain). His early Life (the *Assidua*) and sermons seem to exist only in modern translations:
   check. Plenty of public-domain paintings. Risk: the record is thinner than his fame.
6. **Valentine (603,916).** Hook: "Was St Valentine real?" At least two martyrs of that name are mixed up, and the love
   link begins with Chaucer's *Parliament of Fowls* (about 1380, public domain). Legend only. Risk: a thin record and
   History.com owns the query; it may work better as one sharp myth-busting page before 14 February.
7. **Hildegard of Bingen (563,310).** Doctor, woman, composer, visionary. Sources: F. M. Steele, *The Life and Visions of
   St Hildegarde* (1914, public domain). Pictures: the Scivias illuminations (public domain), which show her at work.
   Risk: few public-domain English translations of her own books.
8. **Bede (291,686).** Hook: "the father of English history", and the man who made "AD" dating common. He wrote the Life
   of Cuthbert, who is already planned, so the research overlaps. Sources: his *Ecclesiastical History* and Cuthbert
   of Jarrow's letter on his death (both public domain). Picture: J. D. Penrose, *The Last Chapter* (1902; the
   painter died in 1932): check the tag. Risk: few scenes outside his death.

## (c) The modern saints: a decision for the owner

Their demand is the largest of all: Carlo Acutis 3,022,975; Mother Teresa 1,633,548; John Paul II 1,305,619; Padre
Pio 676,620; Maximilian Kolbe 561,358; Faustina Kowalska 200,473; Josephine Bakhita 83,721. But the hub's rules were
written for old texts and old pictures. Here is what those rules allow for them.

**Pictures (checked on Commons):**
- **Mother Teresa: yes.** White House photographs of 1981 and 1985 are public domain as US government work, up to
  4000 px. Example: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:President_Ronald_Reagan_Presents_Mother_Teresa_with_The_Medal_of_Freedom_at_a_White_House_Ceremony_in_The_Rose_Garden_-_DPLA_-_57f96756092926aed57839f2338fa7dd.jpg
- **Padre Pio: probably.** Photographs of 1919 and 1947 are tagged public domain (948x1315 and smaller). Check each tag.
- **Kolbe and Faustina: probably.** Photographs of 1936 to 1940 (Kolbe, about 700 px) and 1931 (Faustina, 1409x2274)
  are tagged public domain.
- **Bakhita: one small photograph** (about 1910, 546x546). The uploader also tagged modern paintings as CC0, which
  looks wrong: trust only the old photograph.
- **Carlo Acutis: no.** There is no free photograph of him. Commons holds only photographs of his tomb and relics,
  nearly all CC BY-SA. A page would have no face, which breaks rule 10 ("no placeholder pictures").

**Their own words: almost all in copyright.** Faustina's *Diary* in English (1987), Mother Teresa's letters, Padre
Pio's letters, John Paul II's writings and Carlo's sayings (passed on through his mother's books) are all protected.
Kolbe's Polish writings are public domain in Poland, but every English translation is modern. Under the rule "every
quotation word for word from a public-domain translation", these pages would have no "In his own words" section.

**What a page could still hold:**
- The life in moments, told in our own words from reliable sources (facts are not anyone's copyright).
- How they died, "Is she a saint?" (Catholic only; none is in an Orthodox calendar), the feast day.
- "Often misquoted", which is strong here. Many lines credited to Mother Teresa are not hers (the "Do it anyway" poem
  is Kent M. Keith's, from 1968). The page can name the real source without printing the text.
- The hard topics, which a discernment audience will expect: the critics of Mother Teresa's homes and funding; the
  Vatican's investigations of Padre Pio in the 1920s; the charge of antisemitism in Kolbe's magazine (debated);
  Faustina's *Diary* on the Index from 1959 to 1978; whether Carlo's body is incorrupt (the diocese has said it is not
  intact: check the statement before using it).

**The choice, in plain words:**
1. Keep modern saints out of the hub for now.
2. Allow a lighter "modern saint" page: facts, a free photograph, myths and hard topics, with no quotations (or at
   most one or two short ones, with the source named, if he allows short quotations under fair use).
3. Allow our own translation from a public-domain original (this would only help Kolbe).

If he picks 2, the order would be Mother Teresa, then Padre Pio, then Kolbe. Carlo Acutis stays out until a free
photograph exists, unless he accepts a page without his face.

## (d) Ruled out for now

- **Carlo Acutis, Mother Teresa, John Paul II, Padre Pio, Kolbe, Faustina, Bakhita:** held for his ruling in (c).
- **Saint Stephen (356,212):** a Bible person; he belongs on `/figure/`, like Peter and Moses.
- **Christopher (460,292), Sebastian (446,139), Lucy (403,372), Catherine of Alexandria (291,975), Barbara (214,411),
  Agnes (174,524), Cecilia (173,635):** legend only, with no early record to tell. Better as one group page ("saints
  we know only through legend"), which can also explain why Christopher's feast left the Roman calendar in 1969.
- **Thomas Becket (531,160):** a rich record (Edward Grim's eyewitness account), but the interest is mostly English
  history, where history sites are strong. Next batch.
- **Ignatius of Loyola (364,720) and Francis Xavier (344,256):** rich public-domain records; a natural batch with Philip
  Neri, who is already planned.
- **Bernadette Soubirous (330,015):** public-domain photographs and testimony; a strong next-batch candidate.
- **Brigid (266,871):** legend-thin; her early Lives seem to exist only in modern translations.
- **Julian of Norwich (240,704):** no picture of her exists before modern art (rule 10), and she was never formally
  canonised.
- **Vladimir the Great (228,317):** a violent record and live politics; after Olga, if at all.
- **Bernard of Clairvaux (220,949), Clare of Assisi (212,399), Dominic (143,786):** good records, lower demand. Clare pairs
  with Francis later.
- **Leo the Great (195,821), Helena (177,548), Columba (177,588):** good hooks (Attila, the True Cross, the first Loch
  Ness monster story in Adomnán's Life). Next batch.
- **Edith Stein (165,865):** her writings are in copyright (she died in 1942).
- **John of the Cross (160,224), Kateri Tekakwitha (152,080), Anselm (151,646), Vincent de Paul (148,112), Boniface
  (137,485), Rose of Lima (131,656), Juan Diego (129,288), Martin de Porres (128,667), Francis de Sales (122,461),
  Joseph of Cupertino (121,172):** lower demand; later.
- **John of Damascus (138,057), Cyril of Alexandria (112,289), Maximus the Confessor (85,205), Simeon Stylites (84,986),
  Ephrem the Syrian (82,263), Seraphim of Sarov (65,672), Gregory Palamas (59,085):** lower demand. Ephrem was on the old
  roadmap and suits Lent. Seraphim is the obvious Orthodox saint after 1054, but his English sources are mostly in
  copyright and his portraits are small.
