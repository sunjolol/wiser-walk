Ten pages. One person.

You said the first result felt thin. So researchers went through the Fathers for every honest layer a Christian personality report could carry. Here's what they found. Then the whole report for one example person, the eight types, and what each extra block of questions buys.

What the research found

Gregory sorted people himself. Twice.

He says souls are made of different "dough". One sorting is four dispositions: "one person is of a cheerful, another of a morose, another of a timid, another of a proud disposition." The other is quiet minds and restless minds. Put the two together and you get eight types. Nothing invented.

St Gregory the Dialogist, Moralia on Job

The enemy studies you first

Gregory says the tempter "first beholds the character of each person, and then applies the snares." Pleasure for the cheerful. A quarrel for the gloomy. Fear for the timid. Praise for the proud. So every type gets its own trap.

Same passage

The Orthodox root

Gregory names St Gregory the Theologian as the teacher behind his method. His Oration 2 lists how people need different help: "Some are led by doctrine, others trained by example; some need the spur, others the curb." That becomes a card that tells people how to help you.

St Gregory the Theologian, one of the Three Holy Hierarchs

Where the fight is for you

The eight thoughts of the desert, as St John Cassian taught them. All eight attack everyone. But "every one should discover his besetting sin, and direct his main attack against it." That becomes a private page behind a tap.

St John Cassian, whose teaching is in the Philokalia

Gregory's little grids

Four speeds of anger, from "kindled reeds" that flare and die to "harder kinds of wood" that are slow to light and slow to go out. Four kinds of speakers. Each becomes a simple chart with you on it.

Moralia on Job

Saints whose temper is on record

Sometimes "by nature". Ephrem was quick to anger by nature, and grace changed it. Chrysostom was hot-tempered from his youth. Nazianzen loved quiet "from the first". Martin was never seen angry. And Bible figures: Isaiah volunteers, Jeremiah pleads, Peter rushes in.

Early histories, letters and lives, all public domain

What I left out, and why

No sixteen made-up clusters, because no Father gives them. No "fear, reward or love" score, because it's a ladder everyone climbs and scoring it would grade holiness. No "three powers of the soul" type, because every soul has all three.

How it's checked

Eight researchers read the original texts. Every quote was machine-checked against the saved text. The lines on this page were checked again by me.

Your type

The Still Water

Careful by disposition. Quiet by make-up.

You take things in before you let anything out. You'd rather listen than talk, and you'd rather keep the peace than win. A crowded week wears you down faster than it does most people. Give you quiet and you do your best thinking. People trust you, because you're careful with them.

Where the trap is set for you

Gregory says the tempter studies each person first, then sets the trap right next door. For the cheerful it's pleasure. For the gloomy it's a quarrel. For the proud it's praise. For the careful, it's fear.

"Because the timid dread punishments, he threatens terrors to the fearful."

St Gregory the Great, Moralia on Job

So your temptation rarely looks like temptation. It looks like worry. What if they're upset? What if it goes wrong? What if I say it, and it costs me?

What you bring

  • You listen, and people can tell.
  • You're slow to judge out loud.
  • You rarely make an enemy.

What it costs you

  • You say less than you mean.
  • You carry hurts home with you.
  • You wait to be sure, and sometimes the moment passes.

Saints and Bible figures like you

St Gregory the Theologian

He loved "calm and retirement" from his youth, and ran away when they made him a priest. When Basil called him lazy, he wrote back: "My greatest business always is to keep free from business." Near the end he said, "I am weary of being charged with my gentleness."

When God called him, he pleaded that he was too young to speak. Gregory says he held back out of love: he longed to stay close to God in quiet.

The desert monk who fled the world to pray. In one old story he is seen sailing in silence, with the Spirit of God beside him.

Your opposite, and why you need them

The Herald · St Basil the Great

Confident and restless. Basil and Gregory were best friends for life. Basil pushed and Gregory held back. Basil wanted him in the fight. Gregory wrote back, "Give me before all things quiet." Both are saints.

The desert fathers saw it in a vision: two boats on a river. Arsenius sailed in silence with the Spirit of God. Moses the Ethiopian, who welcomed his guests, sailed with angels feeding him honey.

"One fleeth from the world for Thy Name's sake, and another receiveth and is gracious for Thy Name's sake."

The Sayings of the Desert Fathers

Close call: your second leaning is Serious. A little stronger, and you'd be The Deep Well.

What you might not have noticed

You said you listen more than you talk. You also said you have whole conversations in your head that never happen.

Those are the same thing. The talking doesn't stop. It moves inside, where nobody can answer back. And an argument nobody can answer is easy to win.

You said you let rudeness go. You also said hours later you think of what you should have said.

You win in the moment by staying calm. Then, on a quiet evening, it comes back bigger than it was. The hard part for you isn't staying calm. It's the evening after.

You said you're hard on yourself. You also said a mistake stays with you for days.

Your regret is trying to do the work that mercy is meant to do. Gregory tells people like you to lean on the mercy they're asking for, so the sorrow doesn't crush them.

"They are to be admonished to build upon the mercy which they crave, lest they perish through the force of immoderate affliction."

St Gregory the Great, Pastoral Rule

Where the fight is for you now

This page is private. It shows where the fight is for you right now, not who you are. It never goes on your share card.

Right now: sadness

The desert fathers called it lupe, a heavy sorrow. St John Cassian says it comes in two kinds. One "springs up when anger has died down". The other comes "from unreasonable anxiety of mind". Your answers show signs of both.

How it talks to you

"What ground hast thou to rejoice, when thou endurest so many wrongs from thy neighbours?"

Gregory, Moralia on Job, sadness's own speech

It sounds reasonable. That's how it works.

How it shows

The people you love come round, and everything they say seems badly timed. Cassian wrote it down 1,600 years ago: "we cannot receive with ordinary civility the visits of those who are near and dear to us."

What feeds it

Anger. Cassian's chain runs from anger to sadness, and from sadness to listlessness. Your anger is the slow, lasting kind. Deal with that, and this weakens.

What takes its place

Not cheerfulness. Cassian says its place is taken by "a godly sorrow and one full of joy".

Catch it early

1. It suggestsA thought arrives. That isn't a sin.
2. You enjoy itYou turn it over. It feels good to.
3. You agreeNow it's yours. Catch it at step one.

Your best quality, and its counterfeit

At its best: gentleness

You bear with people. You give them room to be wrong.

Its counterfeit: letting things slide

Gregory warns that "inordinate laxity is believed to be loving-kindness". Letting something go can feel like kindness when it's really avoidance.

Your strength needs a partner

Gregory says every gift needs another beside it. Yours is counsel: you turn things over well. Its partner is courage.

"Counsel is worthless, when the strength of fortitude is lacking thereto, since what it finds out by turning the thing over, from want of strength it never carries on so far as to the perfecting in deed."

St Gregory the Great, Moralia on Job

In plain words: you think it through well. The risk is that you never get round to doing it.

Fed by: your leanings, with no new questions. Sources: Pastoral Rule on vices that pass for virtues; Moralia on the gifts that guard each other.

Who you are in Christ, and in the Body

A gift, or a battlefield. Never a verdict.

St John Climacus says some people are drawn to silence or gentleness by nature. Others fight their own nature to get there, and those who fight stand higher. Your quiet may be a gift you were given. Your patience may be a victory you won. Either way, it isn't the whole of you.

What grace does with a temperament

"Some are possessed by anger, but while they submit this to reason, they convert it into service rendered to holy zeal."

St Gregory the Great, Moralia on Job

Every leaning can be turned to God's use. St Ephrem was quick to anger by nature. After he became a monk, no one ever saw him angry again.

Your place in the Body

"All rules of conduct are not alike. Abraham was a lover of strangers, David was a humble man, Elijah loved silence, and God accepted the work of all of them."

Abba Nastir, The Sayings of the Desert Fathers

Gregory says a quiet mind shouldn't be buried in business. People with your make-up often serve best by listening, praying, caring for one person at a time, and doing quiet work well. Take the spiritual gifts test, and this page will join the two results.

"Let all be eye or all head: will not the body perish?"

St John Chrysostom, on 1 Corinthians

My pick: the full 72. Clearer Thinking says 12 minutes too. In the best study I found, about 2 more people in every 100 quit for each extra 100 questions. So 22 more questions would lose fewer than 1 person in 100. People who are logged in could save and finish later.

Seven answer points: yes. Honestly, the studies say seven points won't make results noticeably more accurate than five, and the best study found no gain past six. But it's what the best tests use, it feels finer, and it adds no questions. What really sharpens results is more questions per trait, and the full version gives that.

  4. How common your type is. Once enough real people have taken it, the report could say "about 1 in 9 people are The Still Water". It would be a real count, never a score against a person. Your rule is never a percentage against a person, so this one is yours to call.
