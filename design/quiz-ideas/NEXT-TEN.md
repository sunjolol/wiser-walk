## 1. The Physician of Souls — "What would a 1,400-year-old doctor of souls prescribe for me?"

Gregory the Great's *Pastoral Rule* (c. 590) says the same medicine cannot be given to every patient, then (Book III)
gives counsel for about three dozen pairs of opposite temperaments: the talkative and the silent, the hasty and the
slow (slothful), the bold (impudent) and the timid (bashful), the stubborn and the changeable (fickle), the joyful and
the sad, the meek and the quick-tempered, those who never start good work and those who never finish it. He
corrects BOTH sides of every pair, so no pole is the "good" one: exactly how the Compass's bipolar engine works
(rails, both poles named). Short everyday scenes, two honest options each. Result: the strongest leanings on rails,
with Gregory's own counsel to people like you, quoted (James Barmby's translation, NPNF 2nd series vol. 12, 1895,
public domain).
- The premise the owner KEPT from the scrapped list; his complaint then was the items (shallow, "nonsensical AI"),
  not the idea. Read Book III whole before writing a single item.

## 2. Your Psalm — "Which psalm am I living right now?"

Athanasius's *Letter to Marcellinus* (4th century) matches states of the soul to psalms (e.g. Psalm 51 for someone
who has done wrong and knows it; Psalm 3 when your own people turn on you; many more). The player describes their
week in a few plain taps (tired, wronged, grateful, restless, waiting); the result is the psalm Athanasius would hand
them, in full (public-domain Bible text), with one line of why. It reads the MOMENT, not a type, so people come back
when their week changes.
- Check each pairing in the letter itself; modern translations of the letter may be copyrighted, so paraphrase his
  prescriptions (they are facts) and cite, and quote only the psalms.

## 3. The Canon Game — "Which ancient Bible would I have put together?"

For centuries churches argued over which books belonged: Hebrews, James, 2 Peter and Revelation were doubted; the
Shepherd of Hermas and the Letter of Barnabas were bound into Codex Sinaiticus (4th c.); 1 and 2 Clement into Codex
Alexandrinus (5th c.). A real passage appears with no name on it; swipe keep or leave (about twelve cards). Result:
the historical list your choices match (the Muratorian Fragment; Eusebius, Church History 3.25; Athanasius's 39th
Festal Letter of 367, the first list of exactly our 27; Sinaiticus; Luther's 1522 order, which moved Hebrews, James,
Jude and Revelation to the back), and what you kept that the Church didn't, and the reverse.
- Much of the text is already held: the Apostolic Fathers are in the Sounds Like Scripture pool (Lightfoot, PD).
- Describe history, never rule on it; keep the deuterocanon question out of it or treat all three canons fairly (the
  site already lets players choose Protestant, Catholic or Orthodox).

## 4. Kindred Spirit and Sparring Partner — "Which early Christian would have been my friend, and which would have written me a furious letter?"

Agree or disagree with real positions in plain words, no names shown until the end: Tertullian's "What has Athens to
do with Jerusalem?" (Prescription against Heretics 7) against Clement of Alexandria calling philosophy a schoolmaster
to Christ (Stromata 1.5); Cyprian against Novatian on second chances for those who denied the faith under
persecution; Chrysostom's "not to share our own wealth with the poor is theft" (On Lazarus 2); Basil asking the
hermit "whose feet will you wash?" (Long Rules 7). Result: the Father nearest you and the one farthest, each with the
real line that shows why. (Jerome really did write furious letters.) Nearest by position, never a percentage.
- Fairness rules apply as on the Compass: each position in words its holder would accept, cited.

## 5. How You Love — "Paul drew love line by line. Which lines are already mine?"

1 Corinthians 13 (approved IN PRINCIPLE by the owner on 2026-09-22, before this list). Scenes with the people closest
to you: a friend gets what you wanted, someone brings up an old hurt, someone is late again; every option is a real
way people act, none labelled. Result: your strongest lines and the one to grow (behind a tap, the rule for any
weakness). Two-person mode: see your portrait beside someone you love's, never a percentage between two people.

## 6. What Kind of Ground — "What happens to a good idea when it lands on me?"

The Parable of the Sower (Mark 4:1-20): the seed snatched away, springing up and withering when trouble comes (no
root), choked by the worries of life, the deceitfulness of wealth and desire for other things (4:19), or taking root
and bearing fruit. Disguised through new hobbies, resolutions, a book that moved you, a busy month. Result: the ground
you are right now and, if thorns, which thorn is loudest. Made for retaking a month later.

## 7. Your Heart's Order — "Going by my choices, what do I actually love most?"

Augustine: "My love is my weight; wherever I am carried, it carries me" (Confessions 13.9.10, "pondus meum amor
meus"); virtue is "the order of love" (City of God 15.22). Plays as a bracket: two good things at a time (a quiet
evening or a finished project, being right or being liked), tap one, about twenty taps. Result: your loves as a
ranked ladder, and Augustine's point that nothing on it is bad; what matters is what sits above what. The bracket is
a way of playing the site does not have yet.
- Keep "God" off the list of goods (it would make every item loaded); the reading comes in the result.

## 8. A Word from the Desert — "Which desert monk would take me as a disciple, and what would he say to me?"

The Sayings of the Desert Fathers (4th-5th c.): people walked into the Egyptian desert and asked the old monks for "a
word". Each Abba and Amma had a temper: Moses the Black carried a leaking jug to a brother's trial ("my sins run out
behind me, and I do not see them, and today I am coming to judge the errors of another"); Arsenius often regretted
speaking, never silence; Agathon kept a stone in his mouth for three years to learn to hold his tongue; Amma
Syncletica, Abba Poemen and others. Questions about noise, silence, people who irritate you, being unseen. Result:
your Abba or Amma, their story, and their word to you.
- Quote from E. A. Wallis Budge's translation (1907, public domain) or the Latin Verba Seniorum; Benedicta Ward's
  1975 translation is under copyright.

## 9. The Letter Written to You — "Seven letters went to seven churches. Which one was mine?"

Revelation 2-3. Each letter follows one form: "I know your works ... but I have this against you ... to the one who
overcomes". Smyrna and Philadelphia get no rebuke; Laodicea gets no praise. Questions about how you are in the
groups you belong to (a team, a family, a church), never church attendance. Result laid out as a letter addressed to
you, the rebuke behind a tap. Very shareable.

## 10. Your Virtue and Its Mask — "What's my best quality, and what does it turn into when I'm careless?"

The other half of the seven deadly sins quiz: the four cardinal virtues (named in Wisdom 8:7; "cardinal" is
Ambrose's word) and faith, hope and love (1 Cor 13:13). Gregory warned that vices pass themselves off as virtues:
stinginess calls itself thrift, extravagance generosity, laxity kindness, anger zeal (Pastoral Rule II.9). Dilemmas
set two goods against each other (justice or mercy, courage or caution), never good against bad. Result: your
leading virtue and the vice that wears its mask for you.
